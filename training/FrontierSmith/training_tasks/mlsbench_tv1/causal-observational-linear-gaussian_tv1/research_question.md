Every classical pipeline for linear-Gaussian structure recovery has a comfort zone: moderate density, unit noise, samples to spare. This benchmark deliberately steps outside it. Two of the five evaluated scenarios are hostile — a denser scale-free graph whose hub nodes must be resolved from only a thousand samples, and a twenty-node graph at edge probability 0.35 with noise scale 2.5 and just four hundred observations — and because the settings are combined by geometric mean, whichever one collapses drags the entire score toward zero. Fixed-significance testing is precisely what collapses there: at low signal-to-noise the tests lose power, the skeleton fills with false adjacencies, precision dives while recall lingers, and SHD explodes.

The question this variant asks: can the accept/reject decisions of a structure learner be calibrated to the data's measured signal-to-noise instead of being frozen constants? Build an equivalence-class estimator whose margin for admitting an edge is a continuous function of quantities it estimates on the fly — sample size, dimensionality, residual noise level, correlation-strength or spectral summaries — such that one configuration serves both the clean 2000-sample scale-free setting and the noisy 400-sample dense one. Hard-coding different behavior per input shape, or any lookup keyed to the scenario table, is out of bounds; adaptation must flow through an estimated statistic.

The bar is stated in the metrics the harness already computes. Adjacency precision on the noisy, dense settings must not crater relative to the easy ones; orientation claims must stay defensible when the skeleton is uncertain, since arrow errors also feed SHD; and SHD must stay controlled everywhere. A method that merely polishes ER10 and ER20 while leaving the hard regimes broken has answered a different, easier question than this one.
