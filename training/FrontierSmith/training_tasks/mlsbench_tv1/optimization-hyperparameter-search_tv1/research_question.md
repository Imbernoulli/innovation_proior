Half of what is measured on every benchmark here is the trajectory, not the endpoint: alongside the best validation score, the harness reports the area under the incumbent-versus-cost curve, and the two are averaged with equal weight before the three benchmarks are combined multiplicatively. These two numbers usually disagree. Greedy early exploitation buys curve area but locks in mediocre finals; patient global exploration wins the endpoint while paying for it with a long flat prefix. The question this variant asks is whether one tuner can make them agree: reach near-final incumbent quality within roughly the first third of the spent budget, and still finish at or above where a final-score-only strategy would.

Fidelity is the intended lever. Cost is charged as the sum of fidelities, so a screen at fidelity 0.25 buys four observations for the price of one — but those observations are biased and noisy (fewer boosting rounds, fewer folds, fewer epochs), and a raw comparison between a cheap score and a full-fidelity score is meaningless. A credible design must therefore commit to an explicit budget split — how much total cost goes to low-fidelity screening versus full-fidelity confirmation — and must treat promotion across fidelities as an inference about bias and noise, not as a lookup of the running max.

Two further constraints. The same strategy, with the same internal settings, must serve all three tuning problems — mixed continuous, integer and categorical parameters, log-scaled ranges, three to six dimensions — with no benchmark-specific branching. And the anytime record must be earned inside the accounting the loop enforces: fidelities are clipped to [0.1, 1.0] and every suggestion is charged against the budget.

Success is a paired claim, made per benchmark: the incumbent curve rises early, and the endpoint gives nothing back — a trade-off that is usually accepted, here surfaced, measured by the run's own trace, and engineered away.
