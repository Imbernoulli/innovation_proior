Additive-noise identifiability is a large-sample, non-Gaussian luxury, and this benchmark's three settings strip that luxury away one piece at a time. One setting uses Gaussian noise — exactly the regime where the additive-noise asymmetry is weakest and where flexible regressors most readily absorb noise into the fitted function. Another provides only 150 observations of twelve variables, where an expressive learner will happily memorize. The third is a 2000-sample scale-free graph that punishes any method whose fitting cost cannot scale. Because the settings combine multiplicatively, the fragile regimes — small-sample and Gaussian-noise — decide the score.

The variant's question: what does nonlinear structure learning look like when every structural claim must survive resampling and every model fit is paid for from an explicit budget? Concretely, the method should operate under a declared cap on the number of regressions it performs, spending that budget where data volume justifies flexibility and falling back to coarser estimators where it does not — the same cap for the 150-sample and the 2000-sample inputs. And it should report an edge, with its direction, only when the identical oriented claim recurs across independent subsamples of the data. Stability gating is the antidote to small-sample overfitting; the budget is the antidote to solving the small settings with machinery that cannot finish the large one. Branching on the observed sample count to dispatch between named algorithms defeats the purpose — adaptation must be continuous, through bin counts, smoothing levels, subsample sizes, or acceptance thresholds derived from the data itself.

Scoring is unchanged: directed-edge F1, precision, recall, and SHD per setting, aggregated by geometric mean. The design target is a method whose low-sample and Gaussian-noise numbers are no longer the weak factors of that product, at unchanged or better cost on the largest graph.
