The number that matters here is not found by the search — it is inherited by a commitment. After the query budget is spent the run must nominate exactly one cell, and the scoreboard records that cell's accuracy on a test split the search never touched, averaged over five seeded runs, with the three datasets combined multiplicatively. Read as a distribution, that scoring is dominated by its bad tails: one unlucky seed drags a dataset's mean down, and one weak dataset drags the product. This variant therefore asks for risk-averse search: maximise the outcome you can guarantee, not the outcome you can hope for.

Two tails need engineering. The first is across-seed dispersion. At this budget a policy's run-to-run scatter is often larger than the gap between algorithm families, and a design in which everything downstream depends on the first few random draws will reliably produce one stranded run in five. Query allocation should be structured — an explicit split between coverage of the space and refinement near incumbents — so that no single early misstep is unrecoverable. The second is mis-commitment. Selection happens on validation evidence but judgment happens on test, and the raw validation argmax is precisely the candidate most exposed to a lucky number. The returned cell should instead be one whose quality is corroborated — by the queried accuracies of its neighbours, by consensus of a surrogate, by the stability of its rank over the run — so that its promise survives the transfer.

The whole design must run unchanged on all three datasets, from the dense high-accuracy plateau of the easiest to the sparse, low-signal landscape of the hardest, and inside the hard ledger the harness enforces: a fixed allotment of budgeted validation queries per run, repeats included, with only the single final test lookup free. The deliverable is a floor — the worst seed on the worst dataset — and the mechanism that raised it.
