Heterogeneous-effect estimators tend to split the verdict: the flexible models that track pointwise effect variation best are rarely the ones with the least-biased average effect, and the tightly stabilized average-effect estimators are heterogeneity-blind. That disagreement is the subject of this variant. Each of the three synthetic settings scores an estimator on two things at once — pointwise accuracy of tau(x) and the bias of its average — and the settings combine by geometric mean, so an estimator that buys pointwise accuracy by tilting its mean, or that nails the average with a flat prediction, caps its own score.

Build an estimator that is ATE-anchored by construction: decompose the problem into a stabilized estimate of the average effect and a heterogeneity component estimated around that anchor, so the mean of the predictions cannot drift far from the best available estimate of the mean effect. Then govern extrapolation with an estimated-overlap diagnostic: in covariate regions where the propensity approaches zero or one — endemic in the 50-dimensional confounded setting — heterogeneous predictions should shrink continuously toward the anchor instead of trusting an outcome model where one treatment arm is essentially unobserved. Note that the economic-outcomes setting is scored on a log scale, so a handful of exploded predictions from inverted propensity weights costs more than uniform mediocrity; tail control belongs in the design, not in a post-hoc clip.

One fixed configuration must serve all three data regimes — 747 samples with 25 covariates and nonlinear surfaces, 2000 with 10, and 4000 with 50 under dense confounding — inside the harness's 5-fold cross-fitting over ten data replications. The success criterion is joint: PEHE and ATE error falling together in every setting, with neither metric purchased at the expense of the other.
