A handful of probabilities attached to *limiting* properties of an independent sequence keep coming out pinned to the ends of the interval — exactly $0$ or exactly $1$, never $0.37$, never $\tfrac12$, never anything strictly inside. A point drawn uniformly from $[0,1]$ has, in its binary expansion, a long-run frequency of $1$'s equal to $\tfrac12$ with probability $1$; the normal numbers have Lebesgue measure $1$ and their complement measure $0$ (Borel 1909). The probability that a series $\sum_n X_n$ of independent terms converges is $0$ or $1$. The strong-law event $\{S_n/n \to c\}$ is $0$ or $1$. Each of these was settled by an argument of its own, and none of those arguments explains the others. Borel, who produced the first of these phenomena, was reluctant to assume countable additivity — he saw no logical absurdity in countably many zero-probabilities summing to something positive, and preferred to exhibit a probability as a limit of finite-trial probabilities — so he had the phenomenon but no infinitary mechanism to account for it. The Borel–Cantelli lemmas (Borel 1909, Cantelli 1916/17) do force $P(A_n \text{ i.o.}) \in \{0,1\}$ under independence, but they do it through a specific criterion, whether $\sum_n P(A_n)$ converges or diverges; that decides one family of events by a computation and says nothing about the convergence of a random series or about the value of $\limsup_n S_n/n$. By the mid-1920s the convergence of a sum of independent variables was being attacked directly — a series of discrete independent terms converges with probability $1$ when the series of means and the series of variances both converge — yet when passing to the limit the probability that $\sum_n X_n$ converges still had to be *defined* by an explicit iterated-limit formula built out of finite-trial probabilities, rather than read off a single measure, which is a sign that the infinitary step had no settled footing. Lévy (1931) isolated further situations, among them the convergence of sums of independent variables, in which a probability can only be $0$ or $1$. So by then the dichotomy was clearly recurrent, clearly tied to independence and to asymptotic events, and still nothing but a list of special results. What I want is the single structural feature responsible, stated in purely measure-theoretic terms, and one theorem proving the dichotomy for *every* event that has it.

The feature is there once one stops looking at the examples one at a time and asks what they have in common. Reach into the digit-frequency event $\{(1/n)\sum_{k \le n} d_k \to \tfrac12\}$ and flip the first ten digits of every outcome: a finite change to the numerator is washed out by the $1/n$, and the event is the *same set of outcomes* afterwards. Prepending finitely many fixed terms to $\sum_n X_n$ cannot make a convergent series diverge or rescue a divergent one. The radius of convergence of a random power series, $R = 1/\limsup_n |X_n|^{1/n}$, is untouched by any finite block of coefficients, because a $\limsup$ ignores one. Whether infinitely many of the $A_n$ occur cannot be decided by the first hundred of them. In every case the event is insensitive to any finite prefix of the sequence: only behaviour arbitrarily far out matters. That is the hypothesis, and it has an exact measure-theoretic form. Writing $\mathcal{F}_n = \sigma(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ for the events built from the first $n$ coordinates and $\mathcal{T}_n = \sigma(X_{n+1}, X_{n+2}, \dots)$ for those built from the coordinates strictly after $n$, an event that ignores the first $n$ coordinates lies in $\mathcal{T}_n$, and an event that ignores *every* finite prefix lies in $\mathcal{T}_n$ for all $n$. So the right object is the intersection
$$\mathcal{T} \;=\; \bigcap_{n} \mathcal{T}_n \;=\; \bigcap_{n} \sigma(X_{n+1}, X_{n+2}, \dots),$$
again a $\sigma$-field, since an intersection of $\sigma$-fields is one. I call $\mathcal{T}$ the tail $\sigma$-field and its members tail events; every example above is one.

The result is the zero–one law: if the $X_n$ are mutually independent, then every tail event $A \in \mathcal{T}$ has $P(A) \in \{0,1\}$, and, as a consequence, every $\mathcal{T}$-measurable random variable is almost surely a constant. The mechanism is a self-reference. Fix $A \in \mathcal{T}$ and any $n$. Since $A \in \mathcal{T}_n$, it is built entirely out of the coordinates $X_{n+1}, X_{n+2}, \dots$, and those are independent of the front block $X_1,\dots,X_n$; so $A$ is independent of the whole $\sigma$-field $\mathcal{F}_n$, for every $n$. The front blocks increase, $\mathcal{F}_1 \subseteq \mathcal{F}_2 \subseteq \cdots$, and together they generate the $\sigma$-field of the entire sequence, $\mathcal{F}_\infty = \sigma(X_1,X_2,\dots)$. If independence from each $\mathcal{F}_n$ upgrades to independence from $\mathcal{F}_\infty$, then $A$ is independent of $\mathcal{F}_\infty$ — and $A$ is itself a member of $\mathcal{F}_\infty$, because $\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_\infty$. An event independent of a $\sigma$-field that contains it is independent of itself, and putting $B = A$ in $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$ gives
$$P(A) \;=\; P(A \cap A) \;=\; P(A)^2 .$$
As sets $A \cap A = A$ is a triviality; independence is what converts that trivial identity into a numerical one, and $t = t^2$ means $t(1-t) = 0$, so the only fixed points in $[0,1]$ are the two endpoints. The open interval is forbidden. That single line explains the whole pattern.

Everything therefore rests on the upgrade step — independence from each finite-prefix $\sigma$-field becoming independence from the $\sigma$-field they generate — and that is exactly where such an argument can quietly fail, since generating $\mathcal{F}_\infty$ involves countable unions, complements and limits, and the product rule has to survive all of it. It survives, for one reason, and the reason can be packaged in two ways. The first uses the extension theorem directly. The events describable through finitely many coordinates form a *field* $\mathfrak{F}$, the cylinder field, whose smallest enclosing Borel field is $B\mathfrak{F} = \mathcal{F}_\infty$; a non-negative completely additive set function on $\mathfrak{F}$ extends to $B\mathfrak{F}$ in exactly one way, the uniqueness coming from the minimal property of $B\mathfrak{F}$. Suppose $P(A) > 0$ (if $P(A) = 0$ there is nothing to prove) and compare two measures: $P$ itself, and the conditional $P_A(\cdot) = P(A \cap \cdot)/P(A)$, which is a probability measure in its own right — non-negative, $P_A(E) = 1$, and completely additive, all inherited from $P$ with $A$ fixed. For $B \in \mathfrak{F}$, $B$ lies in some $\mathcal{F}_n$, so $A$ and $B$ are independent, and the multiplication rule in its Bayes form gives $P_A(B) = P(A \cap B)/P(A) = P_B(A)P(B)/P(A) = P(A)P(B)/P(A) = P(B)$. The two measures agree on the entire generating field; by uniqueness of the extension they agree on all of $B\mathfrak{F}$; and $A$ lies in $B\mathfrak{F}$, so taking $B = A$ yields $P_A(A) = P(A)$, while $P_A(A) = P(A \cap A)/P(A) = 1$. Hence $P(A) = 1$. The second packaging tracks the independence visibly and uses the smallest possible generating families. To check independence of two $\sigma$-fields it suffices to check it on generating families closed under finite intersection, a Dynkin $\pi$–$\lambda$ argument extending it to the $\sigma$-fields they generate. Now $\mathcal{F}_n$ is generated by the $\pi$-system $\{X_1 \le x_1, \dots, X_n \le x_n\}$ and $\sigma(X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+k})$ by the $\pi$-system $\{X_{n+1} \le y_1, \dots, X_{n+k} \le y_k\}$; independence of the variables factorizes the joint probability across the two disjoint index blocks, so every generator of the one is independent of every generator of the other, and $\pi$–$\lambda$ gives $\mathcal{F}_n \perp \sigma(X_{n+1},\dots,X_{n+k})$ for every $k$, hence $\mathcal{F}_n \perp \mathcal{T}_n$ and, since $\mathcal{T} \subseteq \mathcal{T}_n$, $\mathcal{F}_n \perp \mathcal{T}$ for every $n$. Finally $\bigcup_n \mathcal{F}_n$ is itself a $\pi$-system — any two of its members sit in a common $\mathcal{F}_n$, take the larger index, and their intersection is there too — and it generates $\mathcal{F}_\infty$, so independence of $\mathcal{T}$ from every $\mathcal{F}_n$ is independence of $\mathcal{T}$ from that $\pi$-system, and $\pi$–$\lambda$ once more gives $\mathcal{T} \perp \mathcal{F}_\infty$. The upgrade is then honest, and the self-reference returns as before. The two routes are the same fact in different clothes: "unique extension of a measure from a field" and "extension of independence from a generating $\pi$-system" are both the statement that countable additivity pins a measure down from its values on a generating, intersection-closed family.

Each hypothesis is load-bearing, and it is worth saying exactly how. Countable additivity is the lever: every step of the argument transports an equality from the field of finitely-described events out to a limiting event that lives only in the $\sigma$-field, and that transport *is* the unique-extension property. On a merely finitely-additive set function the extension is not unique, the agreement on $\mathfrak{F}$ need not propagate, and a tail event can carry a value in $(0,1)$. This is precisely why the law could not have been stated a generation earlier: refusing countable additivity leaves the phenomenon visible and the mechanism out of reach; once probability is a completely additive set function satisfying the continuity axiom, the mechanism is simply sitting there, and the law is a consequence of the axioms rather than a separate miracle. Independence is equally essential: for a dependent sequence the front block need not be independent of the tail blocks, so $\mathcal{T} \perp \mathcal{F}_\infty$ fails, self-independence is lost, and $P(A) = P(A)^2$ is no longer forced. A Markov chain is the clean negative control — the conditional given the whole past collapses to the conditional given only the present, so the trials are not independent, and the event that the chain eventually settles in one absorbing state rather than another is asymptotic, untouched by finitely many early steps, yet for a chain started off-balance its probability sits squarely inside $(0,1)$. And the tail $\sigma$-field is exactly the right class, no larger: $\{X_1 = 1\}$ for a fair bit has probability $\tfrac12$, and it is not in $\mathcal{T}_1 = \sigma(X_2, X_3, \dots)$, hence not in $\mathcal{T}$, so the law correctly says nothing about it. The dichotomy bites on asymptotic events and only on those.

Two refinements complete the result. The first is that full independence of the $X_n$ is more than the proof consumes. Describe the event by a single function of all the coordinates, $A = \{f(x_1,x_2,\dots) = 0\}$ with $f$ a Baire function — one obtainable from polynomials by iterated passages to the limit, which is exactly the class of $\mathcal{F}_\infty$-measurable functions — and the property "insensitive to a finite prefix" becomes "the value of $f$ is unchanged when only finitely many variables are altered". For such an $f$, knowing $x_1,\dots,x_n$ tells you nothing about whether $f = 0$, i.e. the conditional probability of $\{f = 0\}$ given the first $n$ variables equals its absolute probability. That conditional-equals-unconditional statement is the whole hypothesis the extension-theorem argument needs; independence of the $x_n$ together with finite-change-invariance of $f$ is merely the convenient sufficient condition guaranteeing it. The second refinement pushes the conclusion from events to quantities. If $Y$ is $\mathcal{T}$-measurable — $Y = \limsup_n S_n/n$, or $R = 1/\limsup_n |X_n|^{1/n}$, or the limiting digit frequency where it exists — then $\{Y \le y\} \in \mathcal{T}$ for every real $y$, so the distribution function $F_Y(y) = P(Y \le y)$ takes only the values $0$ and $1$. (That a $\limsup$ is measurable at all is a small check: $\{\sup_{k \ge n} X_k \le x\} = \bigcap_{k \ge n}\{X_k \le x\}$, and $\limsup_n X_n = \inf_n \sup_{k \ge n} X_k$ with $\{\limsup_n X_n \le x\} = \bigcap_m \bigcup_n \{\sup_{k \ge n} X_k \le x + 1/m\}$, all built by countable operations from the $\{X_k \le x\}$.) A non-decreasing, right-continuous, $\{0,1\}$-valued distribution function jumps exactly once, and the jump point is a constant: $Y$ is almost surely deterministic. That is a strong statement about the world — the strong-law limit $\lim_n S_n/n$ is already a fixed number with probability one *before* anyone computes its value, the radius of convergence of a random power series with independent coefficients is almost surely a constant and not a random radius, the long-run frequency of $1$'s in an independent bit stream is a sure constant which Borel's strong law then merely identifies as $\tfrac12$. The law splits every such asymptotic question into a free part, that the answer is deterministic, and a residual part, which number it is; only the residual needs real work.

Stated in the form the proof actually earns, the zero–one law reads as follows. Let $x_1, x_2, \dots$ be random variables on a probability space with completely additive $P$, let $f$ be a Baire function of $(x_1, x_2, \dots)$, and set $A = \{f(x) = 0\}$. Suppose that for every $n$ the conditional probability of $f(x) = 0$ given the first $n$ variables equals its absolute probability,
$$P_{x_1,\dots,x_n}\{f(x) = 0\} \;=\; P\{f(x) = 0\},$$
that is, $A$ is independent of $\sigma(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ for each $n$. Then
$$P(A) \in \{0,1\}.$$
In particular this holds whenever the $x_n$ are mutually independent and the value of $f$ is unchanged by altering finitely many of the variables — that is, for every event of the tail $\sigma$-field $\mathcal{T} = \bigcap_n \sigma(x_{n+1}, x_{n+2}, \dots)$ of an independent sequence; and any $\mathcal{T}$-measurable random variable $Y$ is almost surely equal to the constant $c = \inf\{y : P(Y \le y) = 1\}$.

For the proof, let $\mathfrak{F}$ be the field of all events describable through finitely many of the variables, so that its Borel extension is $B\mathfrak{F} = \sigma(x_1, x_2, \dots)$ and $A \in B\mathfrak{F}$ because $f$ is a Baire function of all the coordinates. If $P(A) = 0$ we are done, so suppose $P(A) > 0$. Take any $B \in \mathfrak{F}$; it involves only finitely many coordinates, say the first $n$ after enlarging $n$ if necessary, so $B \in \sigma(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, and the hypothesis gives $P_B(A) = P(A)$, equivalently $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. Hence
$$P_A(B) \;=\; \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} \;=\; \frac{P(A)P(B)}{P(A)} \;=\; P(B),$$
so the two set functions $P_A$ and $P$ — both non-negative and completely additive, since $A$ is fixed with $P(A) > 0$ — coincide on the field $\mathfrak{F}$. By the uniqueness of the extension of a completely additive set function from a field to its Borel extension, they coincide on all of $B\mathfrak{F}$. Since $A \in B\mathfrak{F}$, taking $B = A$ gives $P_A(A) = P(A)$, while directly $P_A(A) = P(A \cap A)/P(A) = P(A)/P(A) = 1$. Therefore $P(A) = 1$, and in all cases $P(A) \in \{0,1\}$.

For the special case, if the $x_n$ are mutually independent and $f$ is unchanged by altering finitely many variables, then $A = \{f = 0\}$ lies in $\sigma(x_{n+1}, x_{n+2}, \dots)$ for every $n$, so $A$ is independent of $\sigma(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ for each $n$, which is exactly the hypothesis above. In $\sigma$-field language the same thing runs as follows: independence of the variables gives the product rule on the generating $\pi$-systems $\{x_1 \le x_1', \dots, x_n \le x_n'\}$ and $\{x_{n+1} \le y_1, \dots, x_{n+k} \le y_k\}$, which extends by the $\pi$–$\lambda$ theorem to the generated $\sigma$-fields, so $\sigma(x_1,\dots,x_n) \perp \mathcal{T}$ for every $n$; the union $\bigcup_n \sigma(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is a $\pi$-system generating $\sigma(x_1,x_2,\dots)$, whence $\mathcal{T} \perp \sigma(x_1,x_2,\dots)$; and since $\mathcal{T} \subseteq \sigma(x_1,x_2,\dots)$, every $A \in \mathcal{T}$ is independent of itself, so $P(A) = P(A \cap A) = P(A)^2$ and $P(A) \in \{0,1\}$.

For the corollary, if $Y$ is $\mathcal{T}$-measurable then $\{Y \le y\} \in \mathcal{T}$, so $F_Y(y) = P(Y \le y) \in \{0,1\}$ for every $y$. Put $c_0 = \inf\{y : P(Y \le y) = 1\}$ and let $c_1$ be the smallest $y$ with $P(Y > y) = 0$. If $c_0 < c_1$, choose $\varepsilon > 0$ with $c_0 < c_1 - \varepsilon < c_1$; then $P(Y > c_1 - \varepsilon) = 1$, since it cannot be $0$ with $c_1 - \varepsilon < c_1$, while $c_1 - \varepsilon > c_0$ forces $P(Y \le c_1 - \varepsilon) = 1$ as well, and adding the two complementary events gives $P(E) = 2$, which is impossible. If instead $c_0 > c_1$, then $P(Y \le c_1) = 1 - P(Y > c_1) = 1$, contradicting that $c_0$ is the infimum of such $y$. Hence $c_0 = c_1 =: c$, so $P(Y < c) = P(Y > c) = 0$ and $P(Y = c) = 1$: a tail-measurable random variable is almost surely constant. $\blacksquare$

Every one of the motivating phenomena is now an instance with no argument of its own. Convergence of $\sum_n X_n$ for independent $X_n$ is a tail event, since changing finitely many terms cannot change convergence, so its probability is $0$ or $1$. The strong-law event $\{S_n/n \to c\}$ is a tail event and $\limsup_n S_n/n$ is $\mathcal{T}$-measurable, hence almost surely a deterministic constant. The radius of convergence $R = 1/\limsup_n |X_n|^{1/n}$ of $\sum_n X_n z^n$ is $\mathcal{T}$-measurable, hence almost surely constant. The normal-number event $\{(1/n)\sum_{k \le n} d_k \to \tfrac12\}$ for independent fair bits is a tail event with probability $0$ or $1$, and Borel's strong law identifies which. The event $\{A_n \text{ infinitely often}\}$ for independent $A_n$ is a tail event, with the Borel–Cantelli dichotomy the special case in which $\sum_n P(A_n)$ decides between the two values. And the existence of an infinite open cluster in percolation on $\mathbb{Z}^2$ is invariant under changing finitely many edges, hence a tail event of the independent edge variables, hence of probability $0$ or $1$.
