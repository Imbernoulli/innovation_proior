I need a bit that is not already tied to a special algebraic function. A fixed coordinate of the input is hopeless as a universal choice, because a one-way function can simply reveal that coordinate and still be hard to invert. I need to add something to the input so that the bit is easy to compute with the preimage, but any noticeable ability to predict it from the image gives me more than one isolated fact.

I let a fresh random mask choose a parity of the hidden string. For a secret string \(x\) and a mask \(r\), I look at \(\langle x,r\rangle \bmod 2\). For one mask this is only one bit, but as the mask varies it is the whole Hadamard codeword of \(x\). This is the point: a predictor that works on random masks is not just predicting unrelated bits; it is correlated with a structured encoding of the entire secret.

Suppose I have an efficient predictor \(G\) that, on input \(f(x)\) and \(r\), guesses \(\langle x,r\rangle\) with probability \(1/2+\epsilon\). The advantage is over random \(x\) and random \(r\), so I first average. There is a noticeable set of inputs \(x\) for which, after I fix \(y=f(x)\), the function \(r \mapsto G(y,r)\) has noticeable correlation with \(r \mapsto \langle x,r\rangle\). I can focus on such an \(x\), because recovering \(x\) on a noticeable set already contradicts one-wayness.

The tempting recovery method is too brittle. To get \(x_i\), I can use the identity \(\langle x,r\rangle \oplus \langle x,r\oplus e_i\rangle = x_i\). If I ask the predictor on both \(r\) and \(r\oplus e_i\), then xor the two answers, I get a vote for \(x_i\). Let me price that vote. Write the success rate on my fixed \(x\) as \(s(x)=\tfrac12+\delta\); the xor is right only when both queries are right, and a union bound over the two failure events leaves me \(1-2(\tfrac12-\delta)=2\delta\). Run the mental experiment where I am handed a much better predictor, \(s(x)\ge \tfrac34+\delta\): the same bound reads \(1-2(\tfrac14-\delta)=\tfrac12+2\delta\), a genuine majority, and independent \(r\)'s settle \(x_i\) by voting. With the advantage I actually have, \(\delta=1/2p(n)\), the bound is \(1/p(n)\), far below one half, so the vote is consistent with the majority being wrong every time. And I cannot first clean the predictor up by running it repeatedly: its error is not coin-flip noise sitting on top of a correct answer, it is a fixed set of inputs it gets wrong. A \(G\) that answers correctly on a fixed three quarters of the pairs \((y,r)\) and always errs on the remaining quarter has exactly the advantage I am assuming and reproduces its own mistakes on every repetition. The error-doubling has to be removed, not averaged around. I need a way to know one side of the identity without asking the predictor for it.

I can guess a label myself, but only logarithmically many. For a single \(r\), replacing the query \(G(y,r)\) by my own coin costs a factor of two in success probability and nothing else, so one side of the identity is free. The majority, though, needs \(m\) masks with \(m\) polynomial in \(n\), and if I draw those masks independently I have to guess \(m\) labels; all of them land together with probability \(2^{-m}\), which is exponentially small — I would be paying more for the guesses than inverting \(f\) by brute force costs. So the masks cannot be independently drawn. What the voting argument actually consumes is not full independence: to bound the deviation of a sum I only need the summands pairwise independent, and \(m\) pairwise independent masks can be spanned by \(\ell=\lceil \log_2(m+1)\rceil\) seeds. I sample masks \(s_1,\ldots,s_\ell\) and guess the bits \(\langle x,s_j\rangle\); one full set of guesses is correct with probability \(2^{-\ell}\), inverse-polynomial, and I can repeat or enumerate. Now every nonempty subset \(J\) gives me a derived mask \(r_J=\bigoplus_{j\in J}s_j\), and linearity gives the derived label \(\langle x,r_J\rangle=\bigoplus_{j\in J}\langle x,s_j\rangle\). If my seed-label guesses are right, I know \(m\) true labels without querying the predictor.

These derived masks are still random enough. Each \(r_J\) is uniform, and the family is pairwise independent. For coordinate \(i\), I form votes \(\alpha_J \oplus G(y,r_J\oplus e_i)\), where \(\alpha_J\) is my derived guess for \(\langle x,r_J\rangle\). When the seed guesses are correct, the vote equals \(x_i\) exactly when \(G\) is correct on the shifted mask, because \(\langle x,r_J\oplus e_i\rangle=\langle x,r_J\rangle\oplus x_i\). Each vote is therefore a 0-1 variable that equals \(x_i\) with probability \(s(x)\ge \tfrac12+1/2p(n)\), and the votes inherit pairwise independence from the masks, so the sum of \(m\) of them has variance at most \(m/4\) and Chebyshev bounds the chance that the majority tips the wrong way by \(p(n)^2/m\). Choosing \(m=2n\,p(n)^2\), that is \(\ell=\lceil \log_2(2n\,p(n)^2+1)\rceil\) seeds, drives it under \(1/2n\), and a union bound over the \(n\) coordinates leaves the whole string correct with probability at least one half.

Doing this for every coordinate gives me a candidate string. The correct seed-label assignment gives the real \(x\) with noticeable probability; enumerating the assignments, or repeating the randomized choice, gives only a polynomial-size list of candidates. I do not need to know in advance which candidate is right, because I can test them by applying \(f\) and checking which one maps to \(y\).

So the bit is hard-core because prediction becomes inversion. A weak predictor of the random inner product gives me noisy access to the Hadamard codeword of the hidden input. The randomized correlation search turns that weak access into a short list containing the hidden string, and candidate testing converts the list into an inverter. Since the original function is one-way, the predictor cannot exist.
