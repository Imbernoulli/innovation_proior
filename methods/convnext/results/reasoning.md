I start from the comparison that bothers me: a modern hierarchical vision Transformer is beating the residual ConvNet that used to be the default backbone, and the field is tempted to explain the whole gap as self-attention. That explanation is too coarse. The two systems differ in training recipe, stage layout, stem, downsampling, activation choice, normalization choice, block shape, and spatial mixing. If I compare them only as finished systems, I cannot know which difference matters.

So I make the comparison surgical. I keep the small model in the ResNet-50 / Swin-T compute regime, around 4.5 GFLOPs, and I change the ConvNet one family of choices at a time. If the ConvNet remains far behind after I import the non-attention choices, then attention is the likely missing primitive. If the ConvNet catches up without attention, then the system gap was carried by the surrounding design bundle. I do not know in advance which of those two outcomes I will get; the point of the procedure is to find out.

The first confound is training. A plain ResNet-50 trained with the old recipe reports around 76.13 top-1, which is not a fair opponent for a Transformer-era backbone trained with a Transformer-era recipe. So before touching architecture at all, I retrain the same unmodified architecture with the modern recipe: AdamW, 300 epochs rather than 90, 20 epochs of linear warmup, cosine decay, batch size 4096, weight decay 0.05, Mixup, Cutmix, RandAugment, Random Erasing, label smoothing, and stochastic depth. For the stepwise ablations I keep EMA off, because BatchNorm variants are still in the path and EMA interacts badly with them here. I expect this recipe swap alone to close a real, non-trivial slice of the reported gap, since none of it touches architecture yet — it is purely optimizer, schedule, and augmentation. That is the point of doing it first: whatever this retrained number turns out to be becomes the new baseline for every architectural change that follows, so a later architectural gain is measured against an already-modernized opponent rather than against the stale 76.13 figure, and cannot be mistaken for something architecture buys for free.

Now I can change architecture. I begin at the macro level, because compute placement changes the interpretation of everything downstream. The residual baseline has block counts `(3, 4, 6, 3)`. The hierarchical Transformer I am matching has the stage ratio `1:1:3:1`, so I change the block allocation to `(3, 3, 9, 3)`, holding everything else at the retrained baseline and matching the same ~4.5 GFLOPs regime. I don't expect a large swing from this alone — it only redistributes existing compute across stages rather than adding any new operator — but if the new ratio is at least neutral against the retrained baseline at matched compute, that is enough to treat the old `(3,4,6,3)` allocation as an accident of the original residual design rather than something to preserve, and I carry `(3,3,9,3)` into the next step.

Next is the stem. A standard residual stem uses a 7x7 stride-2 convolution and then a 3x3 max pool, producing the initial 4x downsampling by two overlapping operations. The Transformer-style alternative is a patchify projection: one 4x4 convolution with stride 4. Before I trust that swap, I want to be sure the two stems leave the rest of the network with the same spatial grid, otherwise every later stage's resolution and FLOP count would shift and the comparison would be contaminated. On a 224 input, the residual stem does 224 -> 112 (stride-2 conv) -> 56 (stride-2 pool); the patchify stem does 224 / 4 = 56 directly. Both hand stage 1 a 56x56 grid, so they are genuinely interchangeable as 4x downsamplers and only the *manner* of downsampling differs. I replace the stem with the non-overlapping projection, matched to the same compute regime as the previous checkpoint. Because the grid check above already guarantees the two stems are drop-in interchangeable at the resolution level, I expect this swap to be close to accuracy-neutral rather than a source of gain by itself; the bar for keeping it is low — not worse than the overlapping stem — because a simpler, single-operator stem is preferable whenever it isn't worse.

The next question is spatial and channel mixing. A dense 3x3 convolution mixes space and channels together. A depthwise convolution mixes only space, one channel at a time; a 1x1 convolution mixes only channels at a fixed spatial position. That separation looks much closer to the Transformer block, where attention is the token/spatial mixer and the MLP is the channel mixer. I first make the 3x3 convolution depthwise. I want to know roughly how much compute that removes before I run it, so I count multiply-adds for one representative tensor, a 28x28 stage with C = 256 channels. A dense 3x3 with the same in/out width costs `H*W * C * (C * 3 * 3) = 28*28 * 256 * (256*9) = 4.62e8` MACs; the depthwise 3x3 costs `H*W * C * (3*3) = 28*28 * 256 * 9 = 1.81e6` MACs. The ratio is exactly 256 — depthwise is `1/C` of the dense spatial-mixing cost. So depthwise does not just trim a little; it deletes almost the entire convolution's compute. That tells me ahead of time that depthwise alone should shed accuracy roughly in step with the FLOPs it removes — a much smaller spatial-mixing budget is not a free lunch — and that the natural repair is to spend the freed compute back as width. I predict depthwise-alone, matched at the same training budget as the previous checkpoint, comes in below it and at a visibly smaller FLOP count, in line with the ~256x reduction in spatial-mixing MACs; that prediction is the first thing I'd want to check, because if depthwise-alone doesn't fall off the way the count says it should, something about the comparison is off and I would need to understand why before trusting the rest of this axis. ResNeXt already prescribes the second half of the repair — after increasing grouping, widen — so I raise the base width from 64 to 96, matching the tiny hierarchical Transformer width, and predict this recovers most or all of the drop by putting the freed compute back to work, landing close to the retrained-baseline accuracy at a moderately larger FLOP budget than the original dense-3x3 baseline. The move I keep is not depthwise by itself, which the first prediction says should be worse; it is depthwise spatial mixing plus compensating channel width, and the matched-budget comparison between the two is what would decide whether that pairing is real.

I then look at the block shape. A residual bottleneck normally goes wide-to-narrow-to-wide inside the block. A Transformer MLP goes the other direction: hidden dimension is 4 times the input dimension, then it projects back. MobileNetV2 gives the ConvNet analogue with an inverted residual. I use expansion 4, not MobileNetV2's expansion 6, because I am matching the Transformer MLP ratio. The block now has the narrow endpoints connected by the residual branch and a 4x channel expansion inside. Moving to this shape should already lower FLOPs relative to the previous checkpoint on structural grounds alone — the expensive 1x1s that used to sit on the wide shortcut/downsampling path move out of that shape — independent of whatever accuracy comes back. I expect accuracy at worst to hold and plausibly to improve slightly, since the inverted shape is closer to the Transformer block this whole exercise is chasing; the matched-budget accuracy at this checkpoint is what would decide whether expansion 4 (the MLP-matching ratio) earns its place over MobileNetV2's expansion 6.

This inverted shape also tells me where the block's cost now lives, which matters for the next decision. At C = 96, the two 1x1 channel-mixing layers cost `C*(4C) + (4C)*C = 8C^2 = 73,728` MACs per spatial position, while a depthwise 7x7 spatial mixer costs only `C*49 = 4,704`. Channel mixing outweighs spatial mixing by about 16x. So the spatial kernel is the cheap part of the block: I can enlarge it substantially and barely move the FLOP budget. That is the headroom I will use next.

But first the kernel has to be in the right place. In a Transformer block, the token mixer comes before the MLP. In an inverted ConvNet block, the endpoints are narrow and the middle is wide. If the spatial operator is the awkward, FLOP-light one, it should act before the channel expansion, while the tensor is still narrow. I move the depthwise convolution to the top of the block, ahead of the channel expansion. By itself, this reordering is not obviously a win — the depthwise conv now sees the narrow `dim`-wide tensor at every position rather than the `4*dim`-wide one it would see placed after the first 1x1 — so I expect this move alone, matched against the previous checkpoint, to cost a little accuracy if it costs anything, and I do not read a small dip here as a reason to revert. It is the enabling move for the next step, not a final answer in isolation: with the token mixer now sitting on the narrow tensor, and the MAC count above saying the spatial kernel is cheap relative to channel mixing regardless of size, enlarging that kernel becomes nearly free — which is not true if the depthwise conv is left after the expansion.

Now I need to fix the kernel size, and the MAC count says I have room to go large without paying much for it, so I design this as a sweep rather than a single guess: train kernel sizes 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 at the matched budget from the moved-up-depthwise checkpoint, and track both accuracy and FLOPs at each size. My prediction from the count above is that the FLOP curve across that sweep stays close to flat — going from kernel 3 to kernel 11 is a small swing relative to the block's total cost, because channel mixing dominates by roughly 16x regardless of spatial kernel size — so FLOPs should not be the thing that decides the winner. For accuracy, I expect a positive but diminishing-returns curve: some gain moving off kernel 3, which is undersized for capturing anything beyond immediately local structure once the mixer sits ahead of the channel expansion, and I expect that gain to saturate rather than keep climbing as the kernel grows further. My reason to expect saturation specifically around 7 is that the field's Transformer-era competitor at this scale, Swin, uses a 7x7 local attention window; if the useful receptive footprint for a spatial mixer at this stage width is set by that same local scale, kernel sizes past 7 should not have new structure left to capture, and would mostly add parameters and compute without matching payoff. The decision rule is a simple efficiency frontier: keep the smallest kernel size beyond which further increases stop improving accuracy at matched budget, since paying more FLOPs for a size that does not move the metric has no justification. Going in, kernel 7 is my best guess for where that frontier sits, but the sweep is what would actually fix it.

The remaining changes are micro design. First, I replace ReLU with GELU, matching the Transformer MLP's activation. I don't expect this by itself to be a major lever — GELU and ReLU behave similarly over the range most activations sit in, and nothing about the surrounding block changes — so the prediction is a small move either way at the matched budget, and I would read a small move as evidence that activation identity is not where a Transformer block's real advantage over a ConvNet block lives, whatever the sign turns out to be.

The number of activations is a different kind of lever than which activation. The old ConvNet habit is to put an activation after nearly every convolution. A Transformer block has only one activation in its MLP, between the two linear layers. I remove all block activations except the one between the two 1x1 channel-mixing layers, and I expect this to be the largest single move in the micro-design sweep — larger than the ReLU-to-GELU swap — because it tests a genuinely different hypothesis: not which nonlinearity to use, but whether the ConvNet block was ever under-nonlinear to begin with. My working guess is that it was not — that stacking an activation after nearly every convolution, inherited from the pre-Transformer ConvNet habit, was closer to over-saturating the block with nonlinearity than to a necessity, and that a single activation per block, matching the Transformer MLP, should recover accuracy rather than cost it. The decision rule: if removing activations holds or helps at the matched budget, that confirms the over-saturation reading and I keep the sparse-activation block; if it costs accuracy, the ConvNet block needed the extra nonlinearity after all and some of it would have to go back in.

I apply the same pressure to normalization, on the same over-saturation hypothesis. A conventional bottleneck carries multiple BatchNorm layers; I leave one normalization before the first 1x1 layer and remove the others, predicting — by the same logic as the activation sweep — that this holds or helps rather than hurts. I also want to check the tempting extra norm at the very beginning of the block, since a Transformer block does not add one there either; my prediction is that it is redundant with the single interior norm and would not improve the matched-budget result, in which case one norm inside the block is what I keep.

Then I switch the kind of norm. In an unchanged residual ConvNet, replacing BatchNorm with LayerNorm is known to be a bad naive swap — BatchNorm's running statistics suit the original block's wide, densely-activated, multiply-normalized shape. The reason I am willing to revisit that received wisdom here is that the block is no longer that original block: it now has depthwise spatial mixing, sparse activations, and a single normalization, none of which held when the BatchNorm-vs-LayerNorm comparison was first made. My prediction is that in this remodeled block LayerNorm trains cleanly and is at least neutral against the single-BatchNorm checkpoint, because the old failure mode was a property of the old architecture's norm placement and activation density, not an inherent law against LayerNorm in convolutional networks. If that holds at the matched budget, the old received wisdom was scoped to the block it was tested on, not to convolutional networks generally.

The last architectural issue is downsampling. The residual baseline downsamples inside the first block of a stage, with stride in the residual branch and in the shortcut. The hierarchical Transformer uses a separate downsampling operation between stages. The ConvNet analogue is a standalone 2x2 stride-2 convolution between stages, and before committing to trying it bare, I want to check where it could go wrong: with the old in-block downsampling, a normalization layer always sat near every resolution change, because the block doing the downsampling was itself normalized; strip downsampling out into its own bare convolution between stages and none of the surrounding blocks' norms sit at that boundary any more. Resolution changes are exactly where activation statistics shift most abruptly, so an unnormalized boundary there is a plausible instability point — potentially bad enough to stop training rather than just cost a little accuracy. Rather than run the bare version and hope, I preempt it: I add a normalization at every resolution-change boundary — after the stem, before each downsampling convolution, and after the final global average pool — and predict that these boundary LayerNorms are what let separate downsampling train at all, while a bare standalone conv without them is the version most at risk of instability. The test that would confirm this is training both variants at matched budget and checking whether the bare version is measurably less stable across seeds than the boundary-normalized one.

Assuming the boundary-normalized version trains cleanly and holds accuracy at the matched compute regime — which is what the whole chain of predictions above has been building toward — the controlled ConvNet ends this path without ever having added self-attention. That is the answer to the original question this exercise set out to get: if every checkpoint above clears its matched-budget bar the way predicted, the gap between the plain ResNet-50 and Swin-T was carried by the surrounding design bundle — recipe, stage ratio, stem, spatial/channel separation, inverted expansion, kernel scale, activation and norm sparsity, LayerNorm, and normalized downsampling boundaries — not by self-attention itself. Confirming that at the matched ~4.5 GFLOPs regime against the Swin-T reference is the specific comparison that would settle it.

None of the checkpoints above are trustworthy on their own if they only hold at one compute scale — a change that helps a 4.5-GFLOPs network by exploiting some quirk of that particular width and depth would not tell me anything about the ConvNet-vs-Transformer question in general. So the same sequence of design decisions has to be re-run at the larger ResNet-200 / Swin-B regime, around 15 GFLOPs, starting from the same modernized-recipe baseline and applying the stage-ratio change, the patchify stem, depthwise-alone, the width increase, the inverted 4x expansion, moving the depthwise conv up, the kernel-size sweep, GELU, sparse activations, sparse normalization, the BatchNorm-to-LayerNorm swap, and boundary-normalized separate downsampling, in the same order and each matched to the same compute regime as its immediate predecessor. My prediction is that the sign of every step repeats: depthwise-alone should cost accuracy and shed FLOPs at this scale too, widening should recover it, and the inverted shape, sparse activations, sparse normalization, LayerNorm, and boundary-normalized downsampling should each hold or help just as they were predicted to at the small scale. The one place I would expect the larger regime to diverge quantitatively rather than just qualitatively is the kernel-size sweep: at greater width and depth there is more capacity elsewhere in the network to capture long-range structure, so I predict the accuracy-vs-kernel-size curve saturates at or before the same 7x7 point, plausibly even earlier — if the peak shifts down instead of up as compute grows, that is itself informative, since it would mean the useful kernel footprint is closer to a fixed local scale than to something that should keep growing with the model. The two regimes agreeing on sign, even where they disagree on the exact scale of the kernel-size peak, is what would tell me these are real design effects and not artifacts of one particular model size. The decision rule carried through both regimes is unchanged: a design choice earns a place in the final architecture only if it clears its matched-budget bar at both scales, not just the small one.

Putting the design decisions above together — the ones that would survive the matched-budget checks are the ones that make it in — this is the block I am proposing. It begins with a 7x7 depthwise convolution, because spatial mixing should happen before the expansion and should use the kernel size where the sweep is predicted to saturate. It permutes to channels-last, applies one LayerNorm over channels, applies a linear 4x expansion, one GELU, and a linear projection back to the original dimension. Then it multiplies the residual branch by a per-channel learnable scale initialized to `1e-6`, applies stochastic depth, and adds the input. The scale is the LayerScale idea: start each residual branch close to zero contribution, so the network begins near an identity stack and learns the branch magnitude per channel.

Before I trust this block I trace it on a concrete tensor, because the channels-last/channels-first permutes and the residual add are exactly the kind of detail that is easy to get subtly wrong. I feed a `(2, 96, 56, 56)` tensor through one block at dim 96. The depthwise conv keeps it `(2, 96, 56, 56)`; the permute to channels-last makes it `(2, 56, 56, 96)`; the first linear expands the last axis to `(2, 56, 56, 384)`; GELU and the second linear bring it back to `(2, 56, 56, 96)`; the permute back gives `(2, 96, 56, 56)`, which matches the input, so the residual add is well-formed and the block is shape-preserving for any stage width. I also check the LayerScale claim numerically: with `gamma = 1e-6` at initialization, the maximum absolute difference between the block's output and its input over the whole tensor is about `1.0e-6`. So at the start of training the block really is an identity to within the scale of gamma, which is the behavior LayerScale is supposed to give, and confirms why initializing the residual branch near zero lets a deep stack train from a near-identity starting point.

The network around the block is equally specific. The stem is a 4x4 stride-4 convolution followed by channels-first LayerNorm. There are four stages. Tiny uses depths `[3, 3, 9, 3]` and dimensions `[96, 192, 384, 768]`; Small uses `[3, 3, 27, 3]` with the same dimensions; Base uses `[128, 256, 512, 1024]`; Large uses `[192, 384, 768, 1536]`; XLarge uses `[256, 512, 1024, 2048]`. Between stages, the downsampling layer is channels-first LayerNorm followed by a 2x2 stride-2 convolution. After the fourth stage, global average pooling over the last two dimensions is followed by an `nn.LayerNorm` and then the linear classifier.

The implementation details matter because they are easy to get subtly wrong. The block's 1x1 convolutions are implemented as `nn.Linear` after permuting to `(N, H, W, C)`, then the tensor is permuted back to `(N, C, H, W)`. The custom LayerNorm must support both channels-last and channels-first layouts. In channels-last it can call `F.layer_norm`; in channels-first it computes the mean and variance over dimension 1, uses `sqrt(s + eps)`, and applies `weight[:, None, None]` plus `bias[:, None, None]`. The epsilon is `1e-6`. The depthwise convolution uses `kernel_size=7`, `padding=3`, and `groups=dim`. Stochastic-depth rates are linearly spaced from 0 to the requested maximum across all blocks. Convolution and linear weights use truncated normal initialization with `std=.02`, biases are zeroed, and the classifier weight and bias are multiplied by `head_init_scale`.

So the reasoning resolves cleanly into a falsifiable proposal rather than a settled result: if every checkpoint above clears its matched-budget bar in both compute regimes the way predicted, the answer lands on the second of the two original outcomes — attention was never the missing primitive, and the surrounding design bundle was carrying the gap the whole time. The object I am proposing is a pure ConvNet that imports the non-attention parts of the Transformer-era design package: the training recipe, stage allocation, patchify stem, separated spatial and channel mixing, inverted 4x channel expansion, large but not oversized depthwise kernels, sparse activations, sparse normalization, LayerNorm, separate downsampling stabilized by boundary norms, LayerScale, and stochastic depth. I call the resulting family ConvNeXt, and the validation is exactly the matched-budget ladder above, run once at each compute regime against the ResNet/Swin references it is built to be compared with.
