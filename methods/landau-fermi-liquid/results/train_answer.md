Liquid helium-3 at a few millikelvin has a heat capacity that goes linearly with temperature. That is the unmistakable fingerprint of a degenerate ideal Fermi gas — only the fermions within about $k_BT$ of the Fermi surface can be excited, so $C \propto T$ — and yet helium-3 is a *liquid*, bound by an interaction that is not small compared with the kinetic energy. The same thing happens to the conduction electrons in an ordinary metal: a small linear-in-$T$ electronic term, Fermi-gas in shape, wrong in magnitude, even though the Coulomb repulsion between electrons is comparable to their kinetic energy. Only the *slope* is off; in helium-3 the effective mass that fits the measured slope is several times the bare atomic mass. So a strongly interacting system is mimicking a non-interacting one qualitatively while renormalizing the constants quantitatively, and I want to know exactly what survives, what is renormalized, and by how much — without pretending I can solve the many-body Schrödinger equation, because I cannot.

The obstruction is additivity. In the ideal gas $E = \sum_p \varepsilon_p n_p$ with $\varepsilon_p = p^2/2m$ fixed, and the cost of adding one more fermion is $\varepsilon_p$, independent of who else is around. That is precisely the property that dies when the interaction is of order one: add a fermion and it pushes on, and is pushed by, every other fermion, so the energy it costs depends on the entire configuration and there is no fixed band energy to write down. Hartree–Fock tries to rescue additivity by dressing each fermion in the averaged field of the rest, but that keeps only the mean field and throws away the residual interaction between the dressed objects; it repays the omission by giving, for the electron gas, a single-particle group velocity that diverges logarithmically right at the Fermi surface, which is nonsense for a real metal. The medium's response to a moving particle is simply not in the mean field. The other route on the table — perturbation theory in the interaction, as in the weakly non-ideal Fermi gas of Klimontovich and Silin and in Gol'dman's charged electron gas with its plasma oscillations — does deliver both renormalized single-particle energies and collective modes, but only as an expansion in a small coupling, and here the coupling is not small. What I do have is the lesson of He II: the low-temperature properties of a strongly interacting quantum liquid are fixed by the *type* of its excitation spectrum, phonon/roton in that case, and not by the microscopic Hamiltonian. A Fermi liquid ought to be governed by a Fermi-type spectrum in exactly the same way, and that is the level at which I should be reasoning.

I propose the theory of the Fermi liquid: a low-temperature theory built not on the many-body wavefunction but on a *functional of the quasiparticle distribution*, whose first variation is the renormalized spectrum and whose second variation is the entire residual interaction, compressed into a handful of dimensionless numbers on the Fermi surface.

The construction starts by turning the interaction on adiabatically, starting from the ideal gas, and assuming that the *classification* of the energy levels does not change — each gas state goes over continuously into a liquid state carrying the same quantum numbers. This is a strong assumption but a disciplined one: the conserved quantities (total momentum, total spin, particle number) cannot jump under slow, smooth switching, and as long as the system does not actually change state — does not become superfluid, does not open a gap — there is no reason for the level structure to reorganize. It carries its own warning label, since the place where it must fail is exactly the place where the spectrum qualitatively changes, and that is where I would want the theory to break. Granting it, every elementary excitation of the gas — a particle of definite momentum above the filled sea, or a hole below it — maps to an elementary excitation of the liquid with the same definite momentum. I call these *quasiparticles*. They obey Fermi statistics because they are in one-to-one correspondence with the gas excitations, and their number coincides with the number of real particles; a quasiparticle is a real particle dragging along the self-consistent field of all its neighbours.

They are legitimate particles only if they live long enough, and here the Pauli principle does the work for me. A quasiparticle of energy $\varepsilon$ just above the surface decays by creating a particle–hole pair; energy and momentum conservation together with the exclusion of already-filled states force *both* the hole and the promoted particle to lie within $(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_F)$ of the surface, so two phase-space factors squeeze the rate and it goes as $(\varepsilon - \varepsilon_F)^2 \equiv \omega^2$. The lifetime is $\tau \sim 1/\omega^2$, while the excitation's own oscillation period is $\sim 1/\omega$, so $\tau/T_{\rm osc} \sim 1/\omega \to \infty$ as the surface is approached. The excitation oscillates many times before it decays, and right at the Fermi surface it is infinitely sharp. Quasiparticles are well defined precisely *because* the Pauli principle starves their decay. The same counting at finite temperature gives a mutual collision rate $\propto T^2$, hence a relaxation time $\tau \sim 1/T^2$ that diverges as $T \to 0$ — the number behind the observed sound absorption $\propto 1/T^2$, and, as it turns out, the key to a new mode.

Because the dressing of one quasiparticle depends on its surroundings, I am not allowed to write $E = \sum_p \varepsilon_p n_p$. The energy is a *functional* of the distribution, and what I can write cleanly is its variation. Expanding about the ground-state Fermi step in the deviation $\delta n$, with $d\tau = d^3p/(2\pi\hbar)^3$ and the spin spur $\mathrm{Sp}_\sigma$,

$$E - E_0 = \mathrm{Sp}_\sigma \int \varepsilon(p)\,\delta n\, d\tau + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Sp}_\sigma \mathrm{Sp}_{\sigma'} \iint f(p,p')\,\delta n\, \delta n'\, d\tau\, d\tau' + O(\delta^3),$$

$$\varepsilon(p) = \frac{\delta E}{\delta n(p)}, \qquad f(p,p') = \frac{\delta^2 E}{\delta n(p)\,\delta n(p')}.$$

The first variation $\varepsilon(p)$ is the energy change on adding one quasiparticle of momentum $p$ to the *actual* distribution — the Hamiltonian function of the added quasiparticle, no longer a fixed band energy but itself a functional of $n$, hence temperature- and distortion-dependent. The second variation $f(p,p')$ is the interaction between quasiparticles: it says how the presence of one changes the energy of another, $\delta\varepsilon(p) = \mathrm{Sp}_{\sigma'} \int f(p,p')\,\delta n(p')\, d\tau'$. Truncating at second order is not laziness; at low temperature $\delta n$ is confined to a shell of relative thickness $\delta$ around the surface, so the next term is $O(\delta^3)$ and negligible. Two terms is exactly right.

Maximizing the combinatorial fermionic entropy $S = -\mathrm{Sp}_\sigma \int \{ n\ln n + (1-n)\ln(1-n)\}\, d\tau$ at fixed $N$ and $E$ returns the Fermi form $n(\varepsilon) = [e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/\theta}+1]^{-1}$ — but now with $\varepsilon$ a functional of $n$ — and linearizing the dispersion at the limiting momentum $p_0$ defines the effective mass $m^* = p/(\partial\varepsilon/\partial p)|_{p=p_0}$. The heat capacity immediately comes out gas-like with $m \to m^*$, which already explains the helium-3 puzzle qualitatively: the law is linear because it knows only about the Fermi surface, and the slope is renormalized because the slope of $\varepsilon$ is $m^*$, not $m$. At this stage $m^*$ is still only a name for the measured slope; the real content is in $f$.

The simplification that makes $f$ usable is that $\delta n$ is nonzero only on the Fermi surface, so I only ever need $f$ with both momenta *on* the surface. Their magnitudes are both $p_0$, so $f$ can depend only on the angle $\theta$ between them and on the spins, and for spin $\tfrac12$ with time reversal and reflection the only invariants are a scalar and $\sigma\cdot\sigma'$. Expanding each angular function in Legendre polynomials and pulling out the density of states at the surface makes them pure numbers:

$$f(p,p') = f^s(\theta) + (\sigma\cdot\sigma')\, f^a(\theta), \qquad f^{s,a}(\theta) = \sum_l f_l^{s,a} P_l(\cos\theta),$$

$$F_l^{s,a} = D(\varepsilon_F)\, f_l^{s,a}, \qquad D(\varepsilon_F) = \frac{V m^* p_0}{\pi^2 \hbar^3}.$$

These $F_l^{s,a}$ are the Landau parameters, comparable across systems, and the whole strong-coupling problem has been compressed into them.

Now $f$ earns its keep. The cleanest renormalization to compute is $m^*$ itself, and the cleanest handle is Galilean invariance, which holds for a neutral liquid like helium-3: the momentum density must equal the mass current, $\mathrm{Sp}\int p\, n\, d\tau = \mathrm{Sp}\int m (\partial\varepsilon/\partial p)\, n\, d\tau$. Varying both sides in $n$ is subtle, because varying $n$ changes $\partial\varepsilon/\partial p$ *both* directly and through $f$; integrating the extra term by parts in $p'$ and using $\partial n'/\partial p' = -(\hat p')\,\delta(p'-p_0)$ at $T=0$ collapses it onto the surface, giving

$$\frac{1}{m} = \frac{1}{m^*} + \frac{p_0}{2(2\pi\hbar)^3}\,\mathrm{Sp}_\sigma \mathrm{Sp}_{\sigma'} \int f \cos\theta\, d\Omega \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{m^*}{m} = 1 + \frac{F_1^s}{3}.$$

The spin trace kills $f^a$ and the $\cos\theta$ kernel projects out exactly the $l=1$ Legendre component, so the effective mass is not a free fit parameter at all: it is fixed by the $l=1$ symmetric Landau parameter, a property of forward scattering, extractable from a thermodynamic measurement. One caveat I must flag: this argument is valid only where $p$ is the *true* momentum. For electrons in a metal $p$ is the crystal quasi-momentum and the lattice carries momentum too, so this particular relation does not transfer there; for helium-3 it is exact.

The same machinery run on an $l=0$ symmetric distortion gives the compressibility. Since $\mu = \varepsilon(p_0)$, changing $N$ moves the chemical potential in two ways — the kinematic shift of the Fermi level as the sphere inflates, and the interaction feedback through $f$ — and assembling $c^2 = (1/m)(N\,\partial\mu/\partial N)$ yields

$$c_s = \frac{v_F^*}{\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{(1+F_0^s)\left(1+\tfrac{F_1^s}{3}\right)}, \qquad n^2\kappa = \frac{D(\varepsilon_F)}{1+F_0^s}, \qquad v_F^* = \frac{p_0}{m^*}.$$

An $l=0$ *antisymmetric* distortion gives the magnetic susceptibility. A field both Zeeman-shifts $\varepsilon$ by $-\beta(\sigma\cdot H)$ and repolarizes $n$, and the spin part of $f$ feeds back, so the actual shift is self-consistent, $\delta\varepsilon = -\beta(\sigma\cdot H) + \mathrm{Sp}_{\sigma'}\int f\,\delta n'\, d\tau' \equiv -\gamma(\sigma\cdot H)$ with an effective moment $\gamma$ fixed algebraically on the surface. The result is

$$\frac{\chi}{\chi_0} = \frac{1+F_1^s/3}{1+F_0^a},$$

the Pauli value carrying two renormalizations: $m^*$ in the density of states and a Stoner-like enhancement $1/(1+F_0^a)$. For helium-3 the exchange parameter comes out *negative*, about $-2/3$ of the heat-capacity term, so $F_0^a < 0$ and the susceptibility is enhanced. That is a sharp qualitative statement: in the liquid there is no longer any simple relation between heat capacity and susceptibility, because the exchange interaction has driven them apart, whereas the gas has them locked together.

A pattern is now visible, and it is the theory's real content. Every static response is the gas answer, renormalized by $m^*$ (i.e. by $F_1^s$) and by *one* further Landau parameter selected by the symmetry of the distortion — compression picks $F_0^s$, spin picks $F_0^a$, current picks $F_1^s$. And every $1+F$ denominator that appears is a stability boundary: if it reaches zero the corresponding response diverges and the uniform liquid becomes unstable to that particular deformation. The general statement is that the quadratic form $\tfrac12 f\,\delta n\,\delta n$ must be positive for *every* deformation of the Fermi surface; decomposing into Legendre channels, the $l$-th carries a factor $(1+F_l^{s,a}/(2l+1))$, so

$$1 + \frac{F_l^{s,a}}{2l+1} > 0 \quad \text{for every } l, \text{ in both channels.}$$

The $l=1$ case makes it vivid: $F_1^s < -3$ would give $m^* < 0$, so excitations across the surface would lower the energy and the surface would spontaneously deform; $F_0^s = -1$ would send $\kappa \to \infty$. A violation is a spontaneous change of shape of the Fermi surface — precisely the kind of change of state at which adiabatic continuity was always going to fail. The theory thus contains its own domain of validity.

Dynamics needs a transport equation, and here the functional structure bites in a productive way. Because $\varepsilon$ depends on position through $n(r)$, the quasi-classical kinetic equation acquires a self-consistent force even with no external field,

$$\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial n}{\partial r}\cdot\frac{\partial \varepsilon}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial n}{\partial p}\cdot\frac{\partial \varepsilon}{\partial r} = I(n),$$

with $-\partial\varepsilon/\partial r$ the molecular field generated by the other quasiparticles through $f$. Multiplying by $p_i$ and by $\varepsilon$ and integrating gives proper conservation laws, with fluxes $\Pi_{ik} = \mathrm{Sp}\int p_i (\partial\varepsilon/\partial p_k) n\, d\tau + \delta_{ik}[\mathrm{Sp}\int \varepsilon n\, d\tau - E]$ and $Q = \mathrm{Sp}\int n\,\varepsilon\,(\partial\varepsilon/\partial p)\, d\tau$; the isotropic piece in $\Pi_{ik}$ appears precisely because $\varepsilon$ is a functional of $n$, and it is the pressure-like contribution of the field energy.

Now the payoff. Ordinary sound needs collisions to maintain local equilibrium and propagates only for $\omega\tau \ll 1$; but $\tau \sim 1/T^2$, so as $T\to 0$ the mean free path diverges and ordinary sound is absorbed away. Either there is no sound at absolute zero, or there is a mode that does not need collisions. Setting $I(n)=0$, linearizing with $\delta n, \delta\varepsilon \propto e^{i(k\cdot r - \omega t)}$, and noting that $\partial n_0/\partial\varepsilon$ is a delta function on the surface — so the disturbance is entirely a deformation of the *shape* of the Fermi surface, described by the outward displacement $\nu(\hat p)$ — the kinetic equation with its molecular field becomes a self-consistent eigenvalue problem. With $u = \omega/k$, $v = \partial\varepsilon_0/\partial p$, $\eta = u/v$, and $F(\chi) = \mathrm{Sp}_{\sigma'} f \cdot 4\pi p^2 dp/(2\pi\hbar)^3 d\varepsilon$,

$$(\eta - \cos\theta)\,\nu(\theta,\varphi) = \cos\theta \int F(\chi)\, \nu(\theta',\varphi')\, \frac{do'}{4\pi}.$$

This is a propagating mode whose restoring force is the interaction $f$ itself — a collisionless sound. I call it *zero sound*. For a constant kernel $F=F_0$ the right-hand side is $\cos\theta$ times a constant, so $\nu \propto \cos\theta/(\eta - \cos\theta)$, and doing the angular integral, $\int_{-1}^{1} x/(\eta-x)\,dx = -2 + \eta\ln[(\eta+1)/(\eta-1)]$, collapses the self-consistency to a single transcendental dispersion relation

$$\phi(\eta) \equiv \frac{\eta}{2}\ln\frac{\eta+1}{\eta-1} - 1 = \frac{1}{F_0}.$$

Everything qualitative is read off this. An undamped wave requires $\eta > 1$, i.e. $u>v$: the wave must outrun the fastest quasiparticle, or else quasiparticles moving in phase with it absorb it and the logarithm turns complex. $\phi(\eta)$ is positive and decreases monotonically from $+\infty$ at $\eta\to 1^+$ to $0$ at $\eta\to\infty$, so a root with $\eta>1$ exists only for $F_0>0$ — zero sound propagates only if the forward interaction is repulsive. In strong coupling $\ln[(\eta+1)/(\eta-1)] \simeq 2/\eta + 2/3\eta^3$ gives $\phi \approx 1/3\eta^2$ and hence $\eta = \sqrt{F_0/3}$, the speed growing as $\sqrt{F_0}$; in weak coupling $\eta - 1 \sim \exp(-2-2/F_0)$, an exponentially small excess of $u$ over $v$ as the mode barely escapes the quasiparticle continuum (and that limit is more general than the constant-$F_0$ assumption, since a nearly ideal gas has $\nu$ concentrated at small angles and $F_0 \to F(0)$). Zero sound is genuinely a different object from ordinary sound, not a relabelling: ordinary sound is a *rigid translation* of the Fermi sphere, $\nu \propto \cos\theta$, whereas zero sound has $\nu \propto \cos\theta/(\eta-\cos\theta)$, which bulges forward and flattens behind so that the surface changes shape, elongated along the propagation direction. And it is faster — for a nearly ideal gas $u \approx v$ while $c \approx v/\sqrt{3}$. The two live at opposite ends of the $\omega\tau$ scale, ordinary sound for $\omega\tau \ll 1$ and zero sound for $\omega\tau \gg 1$, with strong absorption and no clean mode in the crossover. The same equation, with richer kernels, generates a whole family: an $l=1$ piece $F(\chi) = F_0 + F_1\cos\chi$ admits asymmetric modes $\nu \propto \sin\theta\cos\theta\, e^{\pm i\varphi}/(\eta-\cos\theta)$ obeying $\int_0^\pi \sin^3\theta\cos\theta/(\eta-\cos\theta)\, d\theta = 4/F_1$, whose left side is maximal at $\eta=1$ where it equals $2/3$, so such a mode exists only if $F_1 > 6$; and keeping the spin structure as $K = \tfrac12 F(\chi) + \tfrac12 G(\chi)(\sigma\cdot\sigma')$ reproduces the identical equation with $F \to G/4$, giving propagating spin waves whenever $G$ has the right sign and size.

Finally, the parameters are not free. Inverting the effective-mass and compressibility relations lets me read the Landau parameters straight off measurements, $F_1/3 = m^*/m - 1$ and $F_0 = 3\,m\,m^*c^2/p_0^2 - 1$. For helium-3, $m^* \approx 1.43\,m$ from the low-temperature heat capacity, ordinary sound $c \approx 195$ m/s from the Walters–Fairbank compressibility, and $p_0/\hbar \approx 0.76\times 10^8$ cm$^{-1}$ from the density give $F_0 \approx 5.4$ and $F_1 \approx 1.3$. Feeding those back into the zero-sound dispersion gives $\eta \approx 1.83$, hence a zero-sound velocity $u = \eta\, p_0/m^* \approx 206$ m/s — above the ordinary-sound speed, exactly as the theory demands. That is a concrete, falsifiable prediction: a second, faster sound at the collisionless end, with no hydrodynamic analogue. (Its quanta form a phonon-like Bose branch $\varepsilon = u p$ which contributes to the thermodynamics as $T^3$, a small correction to the $T$-linear quasiparticle part at the lowest temperatures.)

Collected into one statement, this is the deliverable. For a degenerate Fermi system connected adiabatically to the gas, write the energy as a functional of the quasiparticle distribution and expand about the ground-state Fermi step, with $d\tau = d^3p/(2\pi\hbar)^3$:

$$E - E_0 = \mathrm{Sp}_\sigma\!\int \varepsilon(p)\,\delta n\, d\tau + \tfrac12 \mathrm{Sp}_\sigma \mathrm{Sp}_{\sigma'}\!\iint f(p,p')\,\delta n\,\delta n'\, d\tau\, d\tau' + O(\delta^3),$$

$$\varepsilon(p) = \frac{\delta E}{\delta n(p)}, \qquad f(p,p') = \frac{\delta^2 E}{\delta n(p)\,\delta n(p')}, \qquad n(\varepsilon) = \left[e^{(\varepsilon-\mu)/\theta}+1\right]^{-1}, \qquad m^* = \frac{p}{\partial\varepsilon/\partial p}\bigg|_{p_0}.$$

On the Fermi surface $f$ reduces to two functions of one angle, Legendre-expanded into dimensionless parameters,

$$f(p,p') = f^s(\theta) + (\sigma\cdot\sigma')f^a(\theta), \qquad f^{s,a}(\theta) = \sum_l f_l^{s,a}P_l(\cos\theta), \qquad F_l^{s,a} = D(\varepsilon_F) f_l^{s,a}, \qquad D(\varepsilon_F) = \frac{Vm^*p_0}{\pi^2\hbar^3},$$

and the entire low-temperature response of the liquid is the ideal-gas answer renormalized by these numbers:

$$\frac{m^*}{m} = 1 + \frac{F_1^s}{3}, \qquad n^2\kappa = \frac{D(\varepsilon_F)}{1+F_0^s}, \qquad c_s = \frac{v_F^*}{\sqrt3}\sqrt{(1+F_0^s)\left(1+\tfrac{F_1^s}{3}\right)}, \qquad \frac{\chi}{\chi_0} = \frac{1+F_1^s/3}{1+F_0^a},$$

subject to the stability conditions that fix the theory's own domain of validity,

$$1 + \frac{F_l^{s,a}}{2l+1} > 0 \qquad \text{for all } l \text{ and both channels.}$$

Because $\varepsilon$ depends on position through $n(r)$, the quasiparticles obey a kinetic equation with a self-consistent molecular field,

$$\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial n}{\partial r}\cdot\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial n}{\partial p}\cdot\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial r} = I(n),$$

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathrm{Sp}\!\int p_i n\, d\tau + \frac{\partial \Pi_{ik}}{\partial x_k} = 0, \quad \Pi_{ik} = \mathrm{Sp}\!\int p_i \frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial p_k} n\, d\tau + \delta_{ik}\left[\mathrm{Sp}\!\int \varepsilon n\, d\tau - E\right], \qquad \frac{\partial E}{\partial t} + \mathrm{div}\, Q = 0, \quad Q = \mathrm{Sp}\!\int n\,\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial p}\, d\tau,$$

and in the collisionless limit $I(n)=0$ this supports a new mode, a self-sustained oscillation of the shape of the Fermi surface, governed by

$$(\eta - \cos\theta)\,\nu(\theta,\varphi) = \cos\theta \int F(\chi)\,\nu(\theta',\varphi')\,\frac{do'}{4\pi}, \qquad \eta = \frac{u}{v}, \qquad F(\chi) = \mathrm{Sp}_{\sigma'} f \cdot \frac{4\pi p^2\, dp}{(2\pi\hbar)^3\, d\varepsilon},$$

which for a constant kernel has $\nu \propto \cos\theta/(\eta - \cos\theta)$ and the dispersion relation

$$\phi(\eta) \equiv \frac{\eta}{2}\ln\frac{\eta+1}{\eta-1} - 1 = \frac{1}{F_0}, \qquad \eta > 1,$$

with $\eta = \sqrt{F_0/3}$ at strong coupling, $\eta - 1 \sim e^{-2-2/F_0}$ at weak coupling, an asymmetric branch requiring $F_1 > 6$, and spin waves obtained by $F \to G/4$. Applied to helium-3 through $F_1/3 = m^*/m - 1$ and $F_0 = 3\,m\,m^*c^2/p_0^2 - 1$ with $m^*\approx 1.43\,m$, $c \approx 195$ m/s and $p_0/\hbar \approx 0.76\times10^8$ cm$^{-1}$, this gives $F_0 \approx 5.4$, $F_1 \approx 1.3$, and predicts a zero-sound velocity $u \approx 206$ m/s, faster than ordinary sound and waiting to be looked for.
