The distinctive insight of Razborov-Smolensky is to turn an `AC0[p]` circuit from a gate-level object into a low-degree polynomial approximation object over a finite field. Once we choose to work over `F_p`, the `MOD_p` gate aligns with the field's characteristic and admits a low-degree representation; `NOT` is also exact; unbounded fan-in `OR/AND` is handled with a randomized low-degree polynomial approximation. So any small-depth, small-size `AC0[p]` circuit casts a low-degree algebraic shadow at most points of the Boolean cube.

The other half of the lower bound proves that a function like `MOD_q` has no such shadow: when `p` and `q` are distinct primes, the counting period of `MOD_q` cannot be well approximated by a low-degree polynomial over `F_p` on enough inputs. If a small `AC0[p]` circuit computed `MOD_q`, it would produce a low-degree approximating polynomial; but the lower bound on the approximation degree says no such polynomial exists. Hence `MOD_q` is not in `AC0[p]`, and at depth `d` this also gives an exponential-size lower bound, commonly stated as `2^{Omega(n^{1/(2d)})}`.

This is where the proof's pivot lies: instead of analyzing gate by gate how the circuit composes and how it collapses under a random restriction, it first pushes all small circuits uniformly into one algebraic approximation class, then shows the target function is far from that class. Combinatorial structure is replaced by an algebraic shadow, and gate-level complexity is replaced by a degree/approximation barrier.

The method's boundary comes from this same choice. It relies on the finite field's characteristic aligning with the `MOD_p` gate, so it is most natural for prime or prime-power moduli; facing `ACC0` with general composite moduli, there is no equally direct finite-field carrier to use, and this is exactly where the method is powerful but not all-powerful.
