What I want to seize on isn't "yet another AC0 lower bound" — it's that the object of proof has been swapped out. The old gate-level combinatorial analysis would ask: after a random restriction, how does a DNF/CNF or constant-depth circuit simplify, and how does the parity function still retain its complexity? The Razborov-Smolensky move is more radical: first pick a finite field, map the entire circuit into a low-degree polynomial approximation, and then, working in polynomial space, prove that the target function cannot have such an approximation.

The key choice is the characteristic of the field. If the circuit allows `MOD_p` gates, work over `F_p`; that way the behavior of `MOD_p` aligns with the field's arithmetic, and the mod gate is no longer a foreign combinatorial structure but a low-degree algebraic operation. `NOT` can be written exactly as a linear polynomial, and `MOD_p` can be expressed by a constant power of the input sum. The only thing that would blow the degree up is the exact expansion of unbounded fan-in `OR/AND`.

So the proof doesn't aim for exact arithmetization, but for approximation. For a wide `OR` gate, randomly pick a number of input subsets and use whether the sum over each subset is zero to detect "does a 1 exist"; after repeating this several times, the error probability drops exponentially. Composing these approximations layer by layer, the polynomial degree grows with depth, while the total error is controlled by the circuit size and the per-gate error probability. The result: a depth-`d`, size-`S` `AC0[p]` circuit can be simulated on the Boolean cube, at most points, by an `F_p` polynomial of degree roughly polylog in the parameters; the `AC0[3]` special case in the MIT lecture notes writes this as degree `(2t)^d` with error at most `S/2^t`.

The second half turns the proof problem around: if `MOD_q` could also be computed by a small `AC0[p]` circuit, then it too would have the same kind of low-degree, low-error `F_p` polynomial approximation. But when `p` and `q` differ, the periodic structure of `MOD_q` is incompatible with the characteristic of `F_p`; a low-degree polynomial on the Boolean cube doesn't have enough degrees of freedom to track this counting function of a different modulus. So a lower bound on the approximation degree for `MOD_q` rules out small circuits.

This is what it means for an "algebraic shadow" to replace gate-level analysis. The proof no longer tries to describe the combinatorial shape of every possible circuit; instead it shows that the shadow cast by every small circuit falls into a narrow algebraic class of "low degree and approximable", and then shows that the target function's shadow cannot fall into that class. The circuit lower bound is rewritten as: whether some function is far from every low-degree polynomial over the finite field.

This shift has two consequences. The first is power: it goes beyond pure `AC0`, handling circuits with `MOD_p` gates, and gives strong lower bounds for targets like `MOD_q` and the majority function. The second is a boundary: the method depends heavily on the characteristic of the finite field; when the modulus is not a prime power, there is no finite field of matching order available, and a direct generalization to `AC0[6]` or full `ACC0` breaks down. Precisely because of this, it later came to be treated as a canonical example in discussions of natural proofs and the stall in circuit lower bounds: the low-degree/rank/approximation property is very strong, but it still has a clear boundary of applicability.
