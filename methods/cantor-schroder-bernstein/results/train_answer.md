We measure sets by correspondence: $A \approx B$ when a bijection exists between them, and $A \preceq B$ when there is merely an injection $f : A \to B$, an embedding of $A$ into $B$. Reflexivity and transitivity of $\preceq$ are immediate — the identity map, and composition of injections — so the only thing standing between $\preceq$ and a genuine order on cardinalities is antisymmetry: given injections $f : A \to B$ and $g : B \to A$, must there be a bijection $h : A \to B$? For finite sets this is the pigeonhole triviality that $a \le b$ and $b \le a$ force $a = b$. For infinite sets nothing of the sort is available, and the reason is exactly what makes the transfinite interesting: a proper subset can match the whole, as $\mathbb N \approx 2\mathbb N$ via $n \mapsto 2n$, so "injective but not surjective" carries no information whatsoever about relative size. The two injections have to be made to produce the bijection themselves.

Neither given map does the job alone. $f$ is a bijection of $A$ onto its image $f[A]$, but $f[A]$ can be a proper subset of $B$ with no bound on the leftover $B \setminus f[A]$: take $A = B = \mathbb N$ and $f(n) = n+1$, whose image misses $0$. Symmetrically $g$ leaves $A \setminus g[B]$ uncovered. Inverting repairs this in precisely the complementary way: since $g$ is injective, $g^{-1} : g[B] \to B$ is a genuine bijection, and onto all of $B$ — but it is only a partial function on $A$, undefined exactly on $A \setminus g[B]$, the elements with no $g$-preimage. So $f$ is total but not onto, and $g^{-1}$ is onto but not total; each fixes the other's defect where the other fails. Cantor's remaining classical route, well-ordering $A$ and $B$ and comparing order types, does reach the conclusion, but it invokes the Well-Ordering Theorem — that is, the Axiom of Choice — and the bijection it returns depends on the well-orderings chosen rather than on $f$ and $g$. Gluing two correspondences into one is legitimate only when the pieces are pairwise disjoint on both sides, which is a condition to be engineered, not by itself a construction. So the only shape left is the splice: use $f$ on some $C \subseteq A$ and $g^{-1}$ on the rest. The entire problem is *where to cut*, and the cut has to be computed from $f$ and $g$ alone.

The method I propose is the Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein construction: cut $A$ at the Dedekind chain of the $g$-preimage-less seed $A \setminus g[B]$ under $\psi = g \circ f$, and splice $f$ with $g^{-1}$ across that cut. The cut is not guessed — it is forced by a tiling requirement, and that requirement turns into a fixed-point equation. If $h = f$ on $C$ and $h = g^{-1}$ on $A \setminus C$, then for $h$ to be a bijection the two image pieces $f[C]$ and $g^{-1}[A\setminus C] = \{\,b \in B : g(b) \in A \setminus C\,\}$ must tile $B$ with no overlap and no gap. The elements of $B$ outside $f[C]$ form $B \setminus f[C]$, and these must be exactly the $b$ whose $g$-image lies outside $C$; that is, $g[\,B \setminus f[C]\,] = A \setminus C$, or equivalently
$$C \;=\; A \setminus g[\,B \setminus f[C]\,].$$
So the cut solves a self-referential equation: it is a fixed point of the operator $m : \wp(A) \to \wp(A)$, $m(X) = A \setminus g[\,B \setminus f[X]\,]$.

What makes this work is that $m$ is monotone, and the reason is a parity count on order-reversals. If $X \subseteq Y$ then $f[X] \subseteq f[Y]$, since direct image preserves inclusion; complementing flips the direction, $B \setminus f[X] \supseteq B \setminus f[Y]$; direct image preserves the flipped inclusion, $g[B \setminus f[X]] \supseteq g[B \setminus f[Y]]$; and complementing flips once more, giving $m(X) \subseteq m(Y)$. Two complements, two reversals, and they cancel. This is the load-bearing structural fact, and it is easy to expect the opposite — an operator built out of two complements looks order-reversing at a glance, and had it been, the fixed-point route would have been dead on arrival. Because $\wp(A)$ ordered by inclusion is a complete lattice, a monotone $m$ has a least fixed point that can be written down explicitly. Call $X$ *closed* when $m(X) \subseteq X$ and put $C = \bigcap\{X \subseteq A : m(X) \subseteq X\}$. For every closed $X$ we have $C \subseteq X$, hence $m(C) \subseteq m(X) \subseteq X$ by monotonicity; intersecting over all closed $X$ gives $m(C) \subseteq C$, so $C$ is itself closed. Applying $m$ to that inclusion gives $m(m(C)) \subseteq m(C)$, which says $m(C)$ is closed as well, hence $C \subseteq m(C)$. Both directions together: $m(C) = C$. No arbitrary selection entered anywhere; $C$ is pinned down by $f$ and $g$.

An intersection over all closed sets is correct but opaque, so it is worth unfolding what $C$ actually is. Since $f[X]$ and $B \setminus f[X]$ partition $B$, and an injective $g$ carries a partition of $B$ to a partition of $g[B]$, deleting $g[B \setminus f[X]]$ from $A$ leaves exactly the non-$g[B]$ part together with $g[f[X]]$:
$$m(X) \;=\; A \setminus g[\,B\setminus f[X]\,] \;=\; (A \setminus g[B]) \,\cup\, g[f[X]].$$
So $m$ is nothing but the seeded-closure operator $X \mapsto S_0 \cup \psi[X]$ for $\psi := g \circ f : A \to A$ with seed $S_0 := A \setminus g[B]$, and its least fixed point is Dedekind's chain of $S_0$ under $\psi$ — the smallest $\psi$-closed subset of $A$ containing $S_0$, namely $\bigcap\{K : S_0 \subseteq K,\ \psi[K] \subseteq K\}$, well defined because an intersection of chains is again a chain. Both ingredients of that description are forced by the equation rather than chosen. The seed is forced because an element with no $g$-preimage can never be written as $g(\text{anything})$, so it can never lie in $A \setminus C = g[B\setminus f[C]]$ and must belong to $C$. The closure is forced because if $c \in C$ then $f(c) \in f[C]$, so $g(f(c))$ is not among the excluded elements and has to stay in $C$ too. I state the cut as a chain rather than as $S_0 \cup \psi[S_0] \cup \psi^2[S_0] \cup \cdots$ deliberately: the union-of-finite-iterates form presupposes an index set $\mathbb N$, whereas the chain is the more primitive object, defined by closure alone and available before the naturals are. The one identity everything below consumes is Dedekind's structural identity
$$C \;=\; (A \setminus g[B]) \,\cup\, g[f[C]], \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad A \setminus C \;=\; g[\,B \setminus f[C]\,]. \tag{$\star$}$$
It holds by two inclusions: $S_0 \cup \psi[C]$ contains $S_0$ and is $\psi$-closed, because $\psi[S_0 \cup \psi[C]] = \psi[S_0] \cup \psi^2[C] \subseteq \psi[C]$, so it is a chain over $S_0$ and therefore contains $C$; conversely $S_0 \subseteq C$ and $\psi[C] \subseteq C$ give $S_0 \cup \psi[C] \subseteq C$.

With the cut in hand, define $h(x) = f(x)$ for $x \in C$ and $h(x) = g^{-1}(x)$ for $x \in A \setminus C$; all three obligations then discharge from $(\star)$ alone. For totality and well-definedness, $f$ is total on $C$, and off $C$ we need $A \setminus C \subseteq g[B]$ so that $g^{-1}$ applies at all; $(\star)$ puts the seed $A \setminus g[B]$ inside $C$, and complementing gives exactly $A \setminus C \subseteq g[B]$, while injectivity of $g$ makes the preimage unique. For injectivity, a collision inside $C$ reads $f(x) = f(x')$ and gives $x = x'$; a collision inside $A \setminus C$ reads $g^{-1}(x) = g^{-1}(x')$ and gives $x = x'$ upon applying $g$; the only dangerous case is a cross-collision $f(x) = g^{-1}(x')$ with $x \in C$ and $x' \notin C$, and there $x' = g(f(x)) = \psi(x) \in \psi[C] \subseteq C$ by $(\star)$, contradicting $x' \notin C$. The cross case cannot occur because the colliding point drags itself back into $C$. For surjectivity, take $b \in B$ and look at $g(b)$. If $g(b) \in A \setminus C$ then $h(g(b)) = g^{-1}(g(b)) = b$ immediately. If $g(b) \in C$, then $g(b)$ lies in $g[B]$ and so is not in the seed term of $(\star)$, forcing $g(b) \in g[f[C]]$; injectivity of $g$ strips the outer $g$ to give $b \in f[C]$, say $b = f(x)$ with $x \in C$, whence $h(x) = b$. Either way $b$ is hit, and $h$ is a bijection.

The formula is airtight but it does not by itself say *why* this cut is the right one, and there is a picture that does. Take $A$ and $B$ disjoint — rename if necessary, nothing depends on it — and draw an arrow $x \to f(x)$ for each $x \in A$ and $y \to g(y)$ for each $y \in B$. Every vertex has out-degree exactly one, because $f$ and $g$ are total, and in-degree at most one, because $f$ and $g$ are injective. That in-degree bound is the only place injectivity enters the picture, and it is the whole reason the picture is clean. Such a digraph falls apart into disjoint components, and tracing in-arrows backward — at most one predecessor at each step — each component is a cycle, a doubly-infinite path, a path whose first vertex is some $a_0 \in A \setminus g[B]$, or a path whose first vertex is some $b_0 \in B \setminus f[A]$; along any of them vertices alternate $A, B, A, B, \dots$. Using $f$ pairs each $A$-vertex with the $B$-vertex its out-arrow points to; using $g^{-1}$ pairs it with the $B$-vertex its in-arrow comes from. On a cycle or a doubly-infinite path, both pairings are bijections between the component's $A$-part and its $B$-part, so no decision is forced. On an $A$-started path $f$ is forced, because $a_0$ has no in-arrow: $g^{-1}(a_0)$ does not exist, and $a_0$ would be left unmatched. On a $B$-started path $g^{-1}$ is forced, because $b_0$ has no $f$-preimage: under $f$ the $A$-vertices map to $b_1, b_2, \dots$ and the origin $b_0$ is never covered. This is the same $h$. The $A$-vertices whose backward trace halts at an $A$-origin are exactly those reachable forward from $A \setminus g[B]$ along $\psi = g \circ f$ — that is, exactly $C$ — and the rule there says $f$; everything else is $A \setminus C$ and takes $g^{-1}$.

The picture also settles which axioms are being spent. Which of the four types a component has is computed from $f$ and $g$; no representative, well-ordering, or matching is ever produced out of thin air, and on the components where both maps would work the tie is broken by a fixed convention rather than a fresh selection per component. A construction with exactly one outcome for given inputs is what "no Choice" means, so this route needs no Axiom of Choice at all, in pointed contrast to the well-ordering comparison. The single nonconstructive ingredient is deciding, for a given $x$, which type its component has, since the backward trace need not terminate in finitely many steps; that is excluded middle applied to a property of $x$, and nothing stronger.

It is worth being precise about what leastness buys, which turns out to be nothing in the proof: totality used only $A \setminus g[B] \subseteq C$, injectivity used only $\psi[C] \subseteq C$, and surjectivity used only $C = (A\setminus g[B]) \cup g[f[C]]$ — all three are $(\star)$, which is available for *any* fixed point of $m$. Minimality is never consumed, so every fixed point yields a bijection by the identical argument, and the fixed point genuinely need not be unique. On $A = B = \{0,1,2,3\}$ with $f = g$ the $4$-cycle $i \mapsto i+1 \bmod 4$, $m$ is monotone across all $16 \times 16$ pairs of subsets and has four fixed points, $\emptyset$, $\{0,2\}$, $\{1,3\}$ and $\{0,1,2,3\}$; the least gives $h = g^{-1} = [3,0,1,2]$ and the greatest gives $h = f = [1,2,3,0]$, two different bijections, with the two intermediate fixed points producing two more. The freedom is real, and it is exactly the freedom visible in the picture: the least fixed point puts cycles and doubly-infinite paths on the $g^{-1}$ side, the greatest puts them on the $f$ side, and the two canonical bijections — Dedekind's and Zermelo's — differ only on the components where both maps work.

Running the construction on the motivating witnesses confirms it does the sensible thing. With $A = B = \mathbb N$, $f(n) = n+1$ and $g = \mathrm{id}$, the seed $A \setminus g[B]$ is empty, so $C = \emptyset$ and $h = g^{-1} = \mathrm{id}$. Swapping the roles, $f = \mathrm{id}$ and $g(n) = n+1$, the seed is $\{0\}$ and the chain of $\{0\}$ under $\psi(n) = n+1$ is all of $\mathbb N$, so $C = \mathbb N$ and $h = f = \mathrm{id}$; the single element with no $g$-preimage drags all of $\mathbb N$ onto the $f$ side. Both of those are degenerate in that $h$ ends up globally $f$ or globally $g^{-1}$ and the pieces never mix, so the honest test is $A = [0,1]$ against $B = [0,1)$ with $f(x) = x/2$ and $g$ the inclusion. Here the seed is $A \setminus g[B] = \{1\}$, the lone point of $A$ outside $B$; $\psi(x) = x/2$, so the chain is the dyadic orbit $C = \{2^{-n} : n \ge 0\}$, and $h$ sends $2^{-n} \mapsto 2^{-(n+1)}$ along that orbit while fixing every other point. It is injective: the identity off the orbit, a shift on it, and no cross-collision, since every value $2^{-(n+1)}$ is itself an orbit point and so is never equal to an off-orbit input. It lands in $B$, because the troublesome endpoint $1$ is on the orbit and goes to $\tfrac12$, so nothing is left sitting on $1$. And it is onto $B$: an off-orbit $b$ is hit by itself, an orbit point $2^{-m}$ with $m \ge 1$ is hit by $2^{-(m-1)}$, and the one orbit point never hit is $2^0 = 1$, which is not in $B$ anyway. The whole defect is absorbed into an infinite shift down a single orbit, and nothing else moves.

The same map can be reached from two other directions, and each is worth having because they pay for the iteration differently. Dedekind's fundamental lemma packages the result as a statement about sandwiches: if $h_0 : a \to e$ is a bijection and $e \subseteq d \subseteq a$, then $a \approx d$. Take $c$ to be the chain of $a \setminus d$ under $h_0$ and set $h^* = h_0$ on $c$ and $h^* = \mathrm{id}$ on $a \setminus c$; by the same structural identity $c = (a \setminus d) \cup h_0[c]$, the map $h^*$ bijects $c$ onto $h_0[c] = c \setminus (a\setminus d)$ and fixes $a \setminus c$, and $\{c,\, a\setminus c\}$ partitions $a$ while $\{h_0[c],\, a\setminus c\}$ partitions $d$. The theorem reduces to this because $g \circ f$ is a bijection of $A$ onto $g[f[A]]$ with $g[f[A]] \subseteq g[B] \subseteq A$, so the lemma with $a = A$, $e = g[f[A]]$, $d = g[B]$ gives $A \approx g[B]$, and composing with $g^{-1} : g[B] \approx B$ gives $A \approx B$. Here too seeding at $a \setminus d$ is the least fixed point and seeding at $d \setminus e$ is Zermelo's greatest — the same pair of canonical maps reappearing from the same single idea. Bernstein's shell packaging removes the chain machinery entirely and is the most hands-on: set $A_0 = A$, $B_0 = B$, $A_n = g[B_{n-1}]$ and $B_n = f[A_{n-1}]$, which descend, $A_0 \supseteq A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots$. These overlap and so cannot be glued directly; carve instead the disjoint annuli $A_n^* = A_n \setminus A_{n+1}$ and $B_n^* = B_n \setminus B_{n+1}$, on which injectivity transports cleanly, since $f[S\setminus T] = f[S] \setminus f[T]$ for injective $f$ gives $f[A_n^*] = B_{n+1}^*$ and likewise $g[B_n^*] = A_{n+1}^*$. Alternate $A_{2n}^* \xrightarrow{f} B_{2n+1}^*$ and $A_{2n+1}^* \xrightarrow{g^{-1}} B_{2n}^*$, glue, and finish with the cores $\bar A = \bigcap_n A_n$ and $\bar B = \bigcap_n B_n$, on which $f$ restricts to a bijection because injectivity lets it commute with the intersection: $f[\bigcap_n A_n] = \bigcap_n f[A_n] = \bigcap_n B_{n+1} = \bar B$. The price is that the shells are indexed by $\mathbb N$, exactly the dependence the chain formulation was built to avoid. Both assemblies lean on the disjoint-gluing lemma, which deserves its own statement: if the $X_i$ are pairwise disjoint, the $Y_i$ are pairwise disjoint, and $X_i \approx Y_i$ for each $i$, then $\bigcup_i X_i \approx \bigcup_i Y_i$, by taking $h = \bigcup_i h_i$ — well defined because disjointness of the $X_i$ makes the index unique, injective because $h(x) = h(x')$ with $x \in X_i$ and $x' \in X_j$ puts a common value in $Y_i \cap Y_j$ and disjointness of the $Y_i$ forces $i = j$, and onto because each $h_i$ is onto $Y_i$. Disjointness on *both* sides is not decoration: without it two correct bijections can clash on an overlap and their union is not even a function. And at the abstract level the whole thing is Knaster–Tarski: $C$ is the least fixed point of a monotone operator on the complete lattice $\wp(A)$, and the greatest, $\bigcup\{X : X \subseteq m(X)\}$, gives the dual canonical bijection.

Stated the way I would stand behind it, the result and its proof are these.

**Theorem (Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein).** Let $A, B$ be sets. If there exist injections $f : A \to B$ and $g : B \to A$, then there exists a bijection $h : A \to B$. Consequently $A \preceq B$ and $B \preceq A$ imply $A \approx B$, so $\preceq$ is a partial order on cardinalities.

**Construction.** Define $m : \wp(A) \to \wp(A)$ by $m(X) = A \setminus g[\,B \setminus f[X]\,]$, let $C = \bigcap\{X \subseteq A : m(X) \subseteq X\}$, and set
$$h(x) = \begin{cases} f(x), & x \in C, \\ g^{-1}(x), & x \in A \setminus C. \end{cases}$$

**Proof.** $m$ is monotone: $X \subseteq Y$ gives $f[X] \subseteq f[Y]$, hence $B \setminus f[X] \supseteq B \setminus f[Y]$, hence $g[B\setminus f[X]] \supseteq g[B \setminus f[Y]]$, hence $m(X) \subseteq m(Y)$. Therefore $C$ is a fixed point: for every $X$ with $m(X) \subseteq X$ we have $C \subseteq X$ and so $m(C) \subseteq m(X) \subseteq X$, and intersecting over all such $X$ gives $m(C) \subseteq C$; applying $m$ once more gives $m(m(C)) \subseteq m(C)$, so $C \subseteq m(C)$, and $m(C) = C$. Since $f[X]$ and $B \setminus f[X]$ partition $B$ and $g$ is injective, $m(X) = (A \setminus g[B]) \cup g[f[X]]$, so $C$ is the chain of $A \setminus g[B]$ under $\psi = g \circ f$ and satisfies $C = (A \setminus g[B]) \cup g[f[C]]$, equivalently $A \setminus C = g[\,B \setminus f[C]\,]$; call this $(\star)$.

$h$ is total and well defined. On $C$ the map $f$ applies everywhere. On $A \setminus C$, the seed $A \setminus g[B]$ lies in $C$ by $(\star)$, so $A \setminus C \subseteq g[B]$ and each such $x$ equals $g(b)$ for a unique $b$ by injectivity of $g$; thus $g^{-1}(x)$ is defined and single-valued.

$h$ is injective. If $x, x' \in C$ then $f(x) = f(x')$ gives $x = x'$; if $x, x' \in A \setminus C$ then $g^{-1}(x) = g^{-1}(x')$ gives $x = x'$ on applying $g$. In the cross case $f(x) = g^{-1}(x')$ with $x \in C$ and $x' \in A \setminus C$, put $b = f(x)$; then $x' = g(b) = g(f(x)) \in g[f[C]] \subseteq C$ by $(\star)$, contradicting $x' \notin C$.

$h$ is surjective. Let $b \in B$. If $g(b) \in A \setminus C$ then $h(g(b)) = g^{-1}(g(b)) = b$. If $g(b) \in C$, then $g(b) \in g[B]$ so $g(b) \notin A \setminus g[B]$, and $(\star)$ forces $g(b) \in g[f[C]]$; injectivity of $g$ gives $b \in f[C]$, say $b = f(x)$ with $x \in C$, and $h(x) = f(x) = b$.

Hence $h : A \to B$ is a bijection. $\blacksquare$

Only $(\star)$ was used, so the same argument turns *any* fixed point of $m$ into a bijection; the least one is Dedekind's canonical choice and the greatest is Zermelo's. Nothing in the construction well-orders a set or selects an element without a rule, so no Axiom of Choice is invoked; the only nonconstructive step is the excluded-middle decision of whether a given element's backward $f,g$-trace originates in $A$, in $B$, or not at all.
