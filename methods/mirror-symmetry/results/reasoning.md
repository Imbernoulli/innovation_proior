I start by looking at the two natural kinds of deformation a Calabi-Yau admits. One direction is complex structure: changing which local coordinate transformations count as holomorphic. The other is complexified Kahler structure: changing the combined parameter built from the Kahler form and the B-field. Ordinary geometric intuition treats these two kinds of data separately, since the former belongs to complex geometry and the latter to symplectic or Kahler geometry.

The counterintuitive move in mirror symmetry is that it says these two kinds of data can be exchanged between a pair of distinct Calabi-Yau manifolds. The Kahler moduli of `X` need not be studied only within `X` itself; they can instead be studied through the complex moduli of the mirror `X^vee`. Physically, this is two dual descriptions of the same topological string theory. Mathematically, it provides a channel for migrating a problem into a different geometric structure.

The difficulty of enumerative geometry sits precisely on the A-model side of `X`. Counting rational curves, or computing Gromov-Witten invariants, looks like a finite counting problem, but in practice it means dealing with the moduli space of curves, its compactification, singularities, virtual counts, and multiple-cover corrections. Computing directly in the space of curves gets bogged down by geometric complexity at every step.

The mirror translation swaps the target for the B-model of `X^vee`. The B-model does not count curves directly; instead it studies the holomorphic volume form on the family of complex structures and its periods. The period `int_gamma Omega` varies with the complex structure, forming a variation of Hodge structures, and satisfies a Picard-Fuchs differential equation. These objects are still deep, but they are closer to linear algebra, differential equations, and monodromy computations than to directly enumerating curves.

The key point is not simply "the other problem is easier." The key point is that the two are identified as the same quantity by physical duality. The Yukawa coupling on the A-model side contains the Gromov-Witten invariants; the corresponding Yukawa coupling on the B-model side can be computed from period data. I cannot walk the exact dictionary between the two couplings through from first principles in one pass — carrying the underlying field-theory argument out in full turns out to be too hard to execute in practice — so I write down the identification from the physical picture, as an assumption about its form, and treat the resulting series as something to be tested rather than something already proven. Using the mirror map to translate the mirror complex-structure coordinate back into the Kahler coordinate of the original Calabi-Yau, I expand the coupling as a q-series and check it two ways: at the large-complex-structure limit the leading term has to reduce to the ordinary topological intersection number of the space, and the first nontrivial coefficients have to reproduce whatever curve counts are already pinned down by other means. There's a second, sharper check available too: once the lower-degree contributions are stripped out of each coefficient, what's left has to be divisible by a growing integer power (cube of two, then cube of three, and so on) if the assumed form of the coupling is right — that kind of arithmetic coincidence is not something a wrong guess would produce by accident.

This is why the example of the quintic threefold is so representative. Classical Schubert calculus handles the lowest few degrees on its own: the quintic has 2875 lines (degree 1) and 609250 conics (degree 2), and both numbers are already pinned down by direct algebraic geometry before I ever touch the period side. That gives me two checks for free, but two checks are not enough to trust a rewritten problem — anything with the right leading behavior could match two numbers by construction. Degree three is where the identification is actually put on trial: the period series hands me a definite prediction for the number of twisted cubics, and it is a genuinely new number, not one classical geometry already had lying around. When that prediction meets an independent count done the traditional way — direct algebro-geometric intersection theory on the space of degree-three curves, no periods or mirror maps involved — the two numbers disagree. For a moment this looks like exactly the failure I should have expected: an assumption dressed up as a derivation, caught out by a real, rigorous, independently obtained integer. It is not the period-side identification that is wrong, though; going back over the classical computation turns up a bug in the code that carried out the intersection-theory count, and once that is fixed, the two numbers agree. That is the moment the coupling identification stops being a pattern-match against two pre-known integers and becomes something I can actually rely on: it survived a genuine, independent, adversarial check, and the thing that broke was the classical computation, not the translation into periods. Past that point the same series keeps handing me integers — for degree four, five, and beyond — that no contemporary direct method reaches at all.
