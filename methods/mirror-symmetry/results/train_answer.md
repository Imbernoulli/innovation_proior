I understand mirror symmetry as a methodology for relocating a geometric problem, rather than as an isolated theorem. Its core move is to set up a duality between two distinct Calabi-Yau threefolds: the moduli space of complex structures on one space is identified with the moduli space of complexified Kahler structures on the other, mirror, space. At first glance this exchange looks like a classification-level observation, but it is actually a computational strategy, because it translates an enumerative-geometry problem that is hard to handle on one side into a problem governed by Hodge theory, period integrals, and differential equations on the other side.

Concretely, given a mirror pair (X, X^vee), mirror symmetry asserts that the A-model on X and the B-model on X^vee describe the same topological string theory. The A-model is primarily concerned with symplectic or Kahler data, and its typical task is computing Gromov-Witten invariants, i.e. counting rational curves. Handled directly, these objects are very unwieldy: the moduli space of curves needs to be compactified, singularities can appear, and the counting has to account for multiple covers and quantum corrections. The B-model, by contrast, is concerned with complex-structure data; its basic object is the period integral ∫_γ Ω of the holomorphic three-form Ω over three-cycles in singular homology. These periods vary with the complex-structure parameters, forming a variation of Hodge structures, and satisfy the Picard-Fuchs differential equations.

So the computational route undergoes a qualitative change. Rather than doing local counting directly in the moduli space of curves on X, I instead move to the family of complex structures on X^vee, solve the Picard-Fuchs equation to get the periods, choose suitable canonical coordinates near the large complex structure limit, and use the mirror map to translate the mirror complex-structure parameter back into the Kahler parameter of X. From there, I read off the enumerative invariants on the original space from the series expansion of the Yukawa coupling or the prepotential. The hard curve-counting problem is thereby rewritten as a problem of solving differential equations, doing monodromy analysis, and inverting series.

The methodological breakthrough here is that it does not try to patch the original enumerative space with a stronger local technique; instead it directly changes "what counts as a computable object" through dual geometry. On the A-model side, quantum corrections are dense and the moduli space is complicated; on the B-model side, the corresponding quantities are governed by the Hodge theory of the complex structure and can often be computed explicitly via periods and the Picard-Fuchs system. String duality in physics thus stops being merely a statement that two languages are equivalent, and becomes a mathematical machine capable of predicting concrete integers.

The quintic threefold is the most representative historical signal of this strategy. The mirror computation of Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes gave strong predictions for the counting of rational curves of arbitrary degree, at a time when traditional algebraic geometry could only handle a few low-degree cases. These predictions later drove the development of rigorous mathematical frameworks such as Gromov-Witten theory, the mirror theorem, and homological mirror symmetry. This shows that mirror symmetry is not a one-off trick but a new mathematical strategy: translate a hard-to-compute geometric object into a dual space and let the computable structure emerge on its own.

Tightening this methodology down to the quintic threefold gives a completely explicit final form that can be verified order by order without relying on any one-off demonstration program. Take the complex-structure parameter $z$ of the mirror quintic, and expand near the large complex structure limit $z=0$; the fundamental period is the hypergeometric-type series
$$\Phi_0(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(5n)!}{(n!)^5}\, z^n .$$
The Picard-Fuchs equation it satisfies is given by the fourth-order operator
$$L = \theta^4 - 5z(5\theta+1)(5\theta+2)(5\theta+3)(5\theta+4), \qquad \theta = z\frac{d}{dz}$$
The coefficients of $\Phi_0$, $a_n=(5n)!/(n!)^5$, satisfy order by order $n^4 a_n = 5(5n-4)(5n-3)(5n-2)(5n-1)\,a_{n-1}$, which is precisely the explicit statement of $L\Phi_0=0$, and it is the unique solution at $z=0$ that is regular and takes the value $1$.

At $z=0$ (a maximally unipotent monodromy point), $L$ also has three solutions with logarithmic growth, the first of which is
$$\Phi_1(z) = \Phi_0(z)\log z + 5\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(5n)!}{(n!)^5}\,H_n\, z^n, \qquad H_n=\sum_{r=n+1}^{5n}\frac{1}{r},$$
and the other two grow like $(\log z)^2$ and $(\log z)^3$ respectively. The mirror map is given precisely by the ratio of the regular period to this logarithmic period:
$$q(z) = z\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{\Phi_1(z)}{\Phi_0(z)} - \log z\Big) = z + 770\,z^2 + 1014275\,z^3 + \cdots,$$
which translates the complex-structure coordinate $z$ into the complexified Kahler coordinate $q$ on the A-model side: $z\to 0$ (the large complex structure limit) and $q\to 0$ (the large volume limit) are two descriptions of the same cusp in the two geometries — the leading term is simply $q=z$, and every subsequent order is amplified by the quintic's own quantum corrections, rather than $z$ being monotonically compressed into a smaller $q$ as naive intuition might suggest.

The expansion coefficients of the Yukawa coupling in $q$ are precisely the generating function for the Gromov-Witten invariants: it is from exactly this period–mirror-map machinery that Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes read off the counts of curves of degree three and above, alongside the 2875 lines and 609250 conics that classical algebraic geometry had already established. I take the methodological core of mirror symmetry to come down to this: the fundamental period $\Phi_0$ together with the Picard-Fuchs operator $L$ it satisfies, plus the mirror map $q(z)$ given by the ratio of periods, together constitute a final form that translates enumerative-geometry counting entirely into analytic computation — not a piece of demonstration code that needs to be run, but a set of closed-form expressions, handed to us by the dual geometry itself, that can be expanded to arbitrary order. This is exactly the ready-to-use object left behind when mirror symmetry moved from string theory into the core toolbox of enumerative algebraic geometry.
