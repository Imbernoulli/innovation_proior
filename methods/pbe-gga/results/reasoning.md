Let me start from where the pain actually is. In Kohn–Sham theory everything in a real calculation rides on one unknown object: the exchange–correlation energy as a functional of the spin densities, $E_{\rm xc}[n_\uparrow,n_\downarrow]$. I get the kinetic energy of the non-interacting reference exactly, I get the Hartree term exactly, the external potential is trivial — and then there's this one functional that carries all of exchange, all of correlation, and the kinetic correction, and I have to *guess* it. Every atomization energy, every bond length, every barrier height is only as trustworthy as that guess. So the whole game is: write down a usable approximation to $E_{\rm xc}$ that works across the regimes of real matter and gives a clean potential $v_{\rm xc}=\delta E_{\rm xc}/\delta n_\sigma$, because that potential is what goes into the self-consistent equations and into the pseudopotentials people build on top of them.

The cheapest honest thing is the local spin density approximation: borrow the energy per particle from a uniform electron gas of the local density, $E_{\rm xc}^{\rm LSD}=\int d^3r\, n\,\epsilon_{\rm xc}^{\rm unif}(n_\uparrow,n_\downarrow)$. And the remarkable thing about LSD is that it works far outside the regime it was derived for. It's derived for a slowly varying density, but it gives decent numbers for atoms and molecules where the density is anything but slowly varying. Why? Because of the hole. The exchange–correlation energy is the electrostatic self-interaction of each electron with the hole it digs around itself, $E_{\rm xc}=\tfrac12\int d^3r\, n(\mathbf r)\int d^3u\, n_{\rm xc}(\mathbf r,\mathbf r+\mathbf u)/u$, and the energy only sees the spherical, system-averaged hole. The true hole obeys sum rules: the exchange hole integrates to exactly one missing electron, $\int d^3u\, n_x=-1$; the correlation hole integrates to zero, $\int d^3u\, n_c=0$; and the exchange hole is everywhere non-positive, $n_x\le 0$. LSD models the hole by the real uniform-gas hole, which satisfies all three exactly. So LSD isn't a slowly-varying expansion that happens to extrapolate — it's a model that gets the energetically relevant, integrated properties of the hole right. That's the lesson I want to keep in front of me: *the energy is forgiving of the detailed shape of the hole and unforgiving of its integrated constraints.*

But LSD washes out inhomogeneity. It overbinds. Atomization energies come out systematically too big. The obvious next move is the gradient expansion — add the leading $\nabla n$ terms with the coefficients many-body theory hands me, $E_{\rm xc}^{\rm GEA}=E_{\rm xc}^{\rm LSD}+\sum_{\sigma\sigma'}\int C_{\rm xc}^{\sigma\sigma'}\nabla n_\sigma\!\cdot\!\nabla n_{\sigma'}/(n_\sigma^{2/3}n_{\sigma'}^{2/3})$. And here's the wall. The gradient expansion is *worse* than LSD for real atoms. That's almost offensive at first — a systematic correction making things worse — until I go back to the hole. The truncated gradient series is not the hole of any real system. It breaks the sum rules. The gradient-expanded exchange hole isn't even everywhere negative, and at large separation it develops an undamped oscillation, so $\int d^3u\, n_x\ne -1$. I've thrown away the very thing that made LSD work. So I cannot just bolt gradient terms onto LSD.

The repair that the field worked out is to go back to the hole, gradient-expand *it*, and then surgically cut off the parts that violate the constraints — sharply truncate the spurious large-separation tail so that $\int d^3u\, n_x=-1$ and $n_x\le 0$ are restored, then integrate the repaired hole to get a numerical energy as a function of the reduced gradient. That's the real-space-cutoff numerical GGA, and the leading non-empirical fit to it is PW91: take the numerical $f$ from the cutoff procedure and fit it to a closed analytic form that's also been forced to satisfy a long list of further exact conditions.

I've worked with PW91 and I keep tripping over the same things. Let me actually name them, because the list *is* the motivation. (1) The derivation is enormous and depends on a mass of detail — the cutoff radii, the angular integrals, the damping. (2) The function $f$ that fits the numerical cutoff is complicated and nontransparent; you can't look at it and see why it's that shape. (3) It's overparametrized. (4) The parameters aren't seamlessly joined, so the *potential* $\delta E_{\rm xc}/\delta n_\sigma$ develops spurious wiggles at small and at large reduced gradient — and that's not cosmetic, those wiggles bedevil the pseudopotentials you build from the potential. (5) The underlying numerical correlation functional behaves correctly under Levy uniform scaling to the high-density limit, but the *analytic parametrization* doesn't — the fit broke a property the thing it's fitting satisfies. (6) Because PW91 reduces to the second-order gradient expansion whenever the density variation is either slowly varying *or* small, it actually describes the linear response of the uniform gas *less* well than plain LSD does.

That last one stops me. PW91 was built to satisfy as many exact conditions as possible, and yet it's worse than LSD at linear response. So "satisfy every exact condition you can" is the wrong design principle. And there's a reason it has to fail somewhere: the semilocal form $\int f(n,\nabla n)$ is just too restrictive to reproduce *all* the known behaviors of the exact functional simultaneously. There aren't enough knobs in $f(n_\uparrow,n_\downarrow,\nabla n_\uparrow,\nabla n_\downarrow)$ to honor every exact constraint at once. If I force it to honor all of them — which is what fitting to the full numerical cutoff does — I over-contort $f$, and the contortion shows up as the wiggly potential and the broken scaling.

So the principle inverts. Don't satisfy *every* exact condition; satisfy only the ones that are *energetically significant* for real systems, and deliberately let the energetically unimportant ones go. The payoff if I can pull this off: a derivation short enough to follow, an $f$ simple enough to read, a smooth potential, and — if I'm disciplined — no fitted parameters at all, just fundamental constants. That last part matters because a functional with no system-fitted parameters can be applied to anything without re-tuning.

Which conditions are energetically significant, and which are decorative? Two cases settle it. First: in the pseudopotential theory of simple metals, the linear-response limit — how the energy responds to a small density variation around uniform — is *physically* what controls the answer, and there LSD is excellent while the gradient expansion is not. So the linear-response condition is in. Second: recovering the *exact* second-order gradient coefficient in the slowly-varying limit makes essentially no difference to the energies of real systems, because real systems are never globally slowly varying. So the slowly-varying gradient coefficient is *out* — I'll let it be whatever it has to be to serve the conditions that matter. Holding both of those at once is the whole judgment call, and PW91 had them backwards: it nailed the slowly-varying coefficient and paid for it in linear response.

Now, I won't redo the real-space cutoff from scratch — that's exactly the mass of detail I'm trying to escape. I'll invoke only its *general features* (the existence of a smooth enhancement that turns gradient corrections on, with the right limits) and then build the simplest analytic functional that hits the energetically significant constraints directly with constants. Let me do correlation first, then exchange.

Correlation. I'll write it as the LSD piece plus an additive gradient term,
$$E_c^{\rm GGA}[n_\uparrow,n_\downarrow]=\int d^3r\; n\big[\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}(r_s,\zeta)+H(r_s,\zeta,t)\big],$$
where $r_s$ is the local Seitz radius ($n=3/4\pi r_s^3=k_F^3/3\pi^2$), $\zeta=(n_\uparrow-n_\downarrow)/n$ is the spin polarization, and $t=|\nabla n|/(2\phi k_s n)$ is the reduced gradient measured on the Thomas–Fermi screening length — that's the right scale for correlation, because correlation is screened. Here $\phi(\zeta)=\tfrac12[(1+\zeta)^{2/3}+(1-\zeta)^{2/3}]$ is the spin-scaling factor and $k_s=\sqrt{4k_F/\pi a_0}$ is the screening wavenumber, $a_0=\hbar^2/me^2$. (I'll suppress the small $\nabla\zeta$ corrections; they're negligible for present purposes.) The whole job is to pin down $H$, the gradient correction. I'll demand it satisfy exactly the conditions I judge significant.

Condition one — the slowly varying limit, $t\to 0$. Here $H$ has to reduce to its second-order gradient expansion. So
$$H\to (e^2/a_0)\,\beta\,\phi^3 t^2,\qquad t\to 0,$$
with $\beta\simeq 0.066725$, the high-density ($r_s\to 0$) limit of the weakly $r_s$-dependent gradient coefficient for correlation. Why this $\beta$? It's the coefficient that comes out of the many-body theory of the correlation gradient term in the high-density limit, and the same coefficient emerges naturally from the numerical real-space cutoff — so it's a fundamental constant, not a fit. The $\phi^3$ is forced by the spin-scaling: $t$ already carries a $1/\phi$, and squaring it together with the form of the high-density correlation gives the cube. (Note I'm keeping the *form* of the slowly-varying limit, not promising to match the slowly-varying coefficient of the *full* functional — that distinction will bite later, and it's deliberate.)

Condition two — the rapidly varying limit, $t\to\infty$. Picture the tail of a finite system's density, where the gradient is huge relative to the local density. What does correlation do there? Go to the hole. As $t\to\infty$ the correlation hole has to satisfy its sum rule $\int d^3u\, n_c=0$, and in this limit the only way to satisfy it is $n_c\to 0$ — the correlation hole vanishes. In the tail, the exchange energy density and potential dominate their correlation counterparts in reality (unlike in LSD, where they don't). So correlation should switch off:
$$H\to -\epsilon_c^{\rm unif},\qquad t\to\infty,$$
which makes the bracket $\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}+H\to 0$, i.e. $E_c^{\rm GGA}\to 0$. Correlation vanishes in the rapidly varying limit. Good.

Condition three — uniform scaling to the high-density limit. Take $n(\mathbf r)\to\lambda^3 n(\lambda\mathbf r)$ and send $\lambda\to\infty$. Under this, $r_s\to 0$ like $\lambda^{-1}$ and $t\to\infty$ like $\lambda^{1/2}$, and a theorem I trust says the correlation energy must approach a *constant* (it can't diverge). But $\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}$ has a logarithmic divergence in the high-density limit: $\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}(r_s,\zeta)\to (e^2/a_0)\phi^3[\gamma\ln(r_s/a_0)-\omega]$, the Gell-Mann–Brueckner form, where $\gamma$ and $\omega$ are weak functions of $\zeta$ that I'll replace by their $\zeta=0$ values $\gamma=(1-\ln 2)/\pi^2\simeq 0.031091$ and $\omega\simeq 0.046644$. If $\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}$ alone diverges logarithmically and the total has to be finite, then $H$ must *cancel* that logarithm. As $\lambda\to\infty$ I need
$$H\to (e^2/a_0)\,\gamma\,\phi^3\,\ln t^2.$$
That's a strong constraint: $H$ has to go like $+\gamma\phi^3\ln t^2$ at large $t$ to kill the $\gamma\phi^3\ln r_s$ divergence of the uniform piece (using $t\sim\lambda^{1/2}$, $r_s\sim\lambda^{-1}$, so $\ln t^2\sim -\ln r_s$ up to constants — the signs line up to cancel). This is precisely the condition PW91's analytic parametrization violates (problem 5). I want it satisfied *by construction*.

So I need one function $H(t)$ that does three things: starts as $\beta\phi^3 t^2$ at small $t$, saturates at $-\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}$ as $t\to\infty$, and along the high-density scaling path behaves as $\gamma\phi^3\ln t^2$. The cleanest object that turns a quadratic into a logarithm is $\ln(1+x)$: it's $\approx x$ for small $x$ and $\approx\ln x$ for large $x$. So try
$$H=(e^2/a_0)\,\gamma\,\phi^3\,\ln\!\Big\{1+\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\,t^2\,\big[\text{something}(t)\big]\Big\}.$$
Check the small-$t$ end first. With the bracket $\to 1$ and $\ln(1+x)\approx x$, $H\approx(e^2/a_0)\gamma\phi^3\cdot(\beta/\gamma)t^2=(e^2/a_0)\beta\phi^3 t^2$. That's condition one, exactly, with the $\gamma$'s cancelling — beautiful, that's *why* I factored $\beta/\gamma$ out front. So the outer $\gamma\phi^3\ln(\cdots)$ gives me the high-density log for free and the inner $(\beta/\gamma)t^2$ gives me the slowly-varying $\beta$ coefficient for free. The only remaining job is the bracket: make $H$ saturate at $-\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}$ as $t\to\infty$.

Let me find what "something$(t)$" has to be. As $t\to\infty$ I want the whole argument of the log to become independent of $t$ and equal to the value that makes $H=-\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}$. Set $H_\infty=-\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}$; then $\ln(1+\text{arg}_\infty)=-\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}/(\gamma\phi^3 e^2/a_0)$, so $\text{arg}_\infty=\exp[-\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}/(\gamma\phi^3 e^2/a_0)]-1$. Define
$$A\equiv\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\Big[\exp\!\big\{-\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}/(\gamma\phi^3 e^2/a_0)\big\}-1\Big]^{-1},$$
so that $\beta/\gamma=A\cdot(\text{arg}_\infty)$. Now I want a rational function of $t$ inside such that the argument $\to\text{arg}_\infty$ at large $t$ and the leading small-$t$ piece is $(\beta/\gamma)t^2$. The form
$$H=(e^2/a_0)\,\gamma\,\phi^3\,\ln\!\Big\{1+\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\,t^2\,\frac{1+At^2}{1+At^2+A^2t^4}\Big\}$$
does it. Walk the limits. Small $t$: the fraction $(1+At^2)/(1+At^2+A^2t^4)\to 1$, so the argument $\to(\beta/\gamma)t^2$ — condition one holds. Large $t$: the fraction $\to At^2/(A^2t^4)=1/(At^2)$, so $(\beta/\gamma)t^2\cdot 1/(At^2)=(\beta/\gamma)/A=\text{arg}_\infty$ by the definition of $A$ — so $H\to(e^2/a_0)\gamma\phi^3\ln(1+\text{arg}_\infty)=-\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}$, condition two holds, with the bracket frozen at exactly the right constant. And what about the high-density scaling path, condition three? There I send $r_s\to 0$ ($\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}\to-\infty$ logarithmically, $A\to\infty$) with $t\to\infty$; in that regime the argument of the log grows like $t^2$ and $H\to(e^2/a_0)\gamma\phi^3\ln t^2$ — the log term I need to cancel the uniform divergence. All three conditions, from one compact ansatz. The bracket $\frac{1+At^2}{1+At^2+A^2t^4}$ is doing exactly one job: it's the interpolation that lets the same expression read as $\beta t^2$ early, $\ln t^2$ along the scaling path, and a frozen constant at the rapidly-varying end.

Let me sanity check the monotonicity and sign. $H$ starts from zero like $\beta\phi^3 t^2>0$ at $t=0$ and grows monotonically up to $-\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}\ge 0$ as $t\to\infty$, so the bracket $\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}+H$ runs from $\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}<0$ up to $0$ — meaning $E_c^{\rm GGA}\le 0$ always, as it must be. (This same $H$ is one of the two terms in PW91's correlation energy, but PW91 used $\gamma=0.025$ rather than the high-density-correct $(1-\ln2)/\pi^2$; that's part of why PW91's scaling came out wrong.)

One more check on the high-density limit, because I want to *see* it come out finite. Under scaling to high density the whole correlation energy tends to
$$E_c^{\rm GGA}\to -\frac{e^2}{a_0}\int d^3r\, n\,\gamma\,\phi^3\ln\!\Big[1+\frac{1}{\chi s^2/\phi^2+(\chi s^2/\phi^2)^2}\Big],$$
where $s=|\nabla n|/(2k_F n)=(r_s/a_0)^{1/2}\phi t/c$ is the *other* reduced gradient (on the Fermi-wavelength scale), $c=(3\pi^2/16)^{1/3}\simeq 1.2277$, and $\chi=(\beta/\gamma)c^2\exp(-\omega/\gamma)\simeq 0.72161$. This is finite for any system where $s$ doesn't vanish identically — which is every finite system, since $s=0$ only on sets of measure zero. For infinite jellium $s\to 0$ everywhere and the bracket blows up logarithmically, sending $E_c^{\rm GGA}\to -\infty$, i.e. GGA collapses to LSD — exactly right, jellium *is* the uniform gas. As a numerical touchstone: the correlation energy of a two-electron ion as the nuclear charge $Z\to\infty$ comes out $-0.0482$ Hartree from this expression, against $-0.0467$ exact, where LSD gives $-\infty$ and PW91's parametrization gives $+\infty$. So the construction is doing the high-density physics that the old fit got qualitatively wrong.

Now exchange. The structure is the same idea — a local exchange energy times an enhancement factor — but the conditions are different because exchange has its own exact properties. Write
$$E_x^{\rm GGA}=\int d^3r\; n\,\epsilon_x^{\rm unif}(n)\,F_x(s),\qquad \epsilon_x^{\rm unif}=-\frac{3e^2k_F}{4\pi},$$
with $s$ the Fermi-wavelength reduced gradient (the right scale for exchange — exchange is unscreened, set by $k_F$). I need to justify why exchange depends only on $s$ and not on $r_s$ separately. That's condition (d): under uniform scaling exchange is *exactly* homogeneous of degree one, $E_x[\lambda^3 n(\lambda\mathbf r)]=\lambda E_x[n]$. The local exchange $\int n\,\epsilon_x^{\rm unif}(n)$ already scales correctly, so the enhancement must be scale-invariant, a function of a scale-invariant variable — and $s=|\nabla n|/(2k_F n)$ is exactly that (numerator and denominator both scale as $\lambda^4$). So $F_x=F_x(s)$, full stop, and to recover the uniform gas $F_x(0)=1$.

Condition (e): the exact exchange energy obeys the spin-scaling relation $E_x[n_\uparrow,n_\downarrow]=\tfrac12(E_x[2n_\uparrow]+E_x[2n_\downarrow])$. This is exact — exchange of a spin-polarized system is the average of fully-polarized exchanges at doubled densities. It means I only ever have to model spin-unpolarized exchange and the polarized case follows. So I build $F_x(s)$ for $\zeta=0$ and let spin-scaling generate the rest.

Condition (f) — and this is the one I have to get philosophically right. For the linear response of the spin-unpolarized uniform gas — small density variations around uniform — LSD is an excellent approximation to $E_{\rm xc}$, while the gradient expansion is not. I decided this condition is energetically significant. So as $s\to 0$ I need the *exchange* enhancement to recover the LSD linear response, which means
$$F_x(s)\to 1+\mu s^2,\qquad s\to 0,$$
where — and here's the subtle part — $\mu=\beta(\pi^2/3)\simeq 0.21951$ is chosen so that the effective gradient coefficient for *exchange* cancels the one for *correlation* in the linear-response regime. Let me make sure I see why this is the right $\mu$ and not the "true" gradient-expansion exchange coefficient. The genuine second-order gradient coefficient for exchange, from the slowly-varying expansion, is $\mu_{\rm GE}=10/81\approx 0.1235$. If I used *that*, I'd match the slowly-varying limit of exchange exactly — but I already decided the slowly-varying coefficient is *not* energetically significant. What *is* significant is the linear response, and for the linear response of $E_{\rm xc}$ to come out right (matching LSD, which is excellent there) the exchange gradient term has to cancel against the correlation gradient term. The correlation side brought $\beta$; setting $\mu=\beta\pi^2/3$ makes the exchange contribution to the small-$s$ response cancel the correlation contribution, restoring the LSD linear response. Numerically $\mu=0.2195$ is close to $2\mu_{\rm GE}$, almost twice the slowly-varying value — so I am *deliberately* using the wrong slowly-varying coefficient in order to get the linear response (the thing that matters) right. That's the inversion of PW91's priorities, made concrete in a single constant. I'm spending the slowly-varying gradient coefficient — a thing real systems barely feel — to buy the linear response — a thing simple metals genuinely feel.

Condition (g) — the Lieb–Oxford bound. There's a rigorous lower bound, $E_{\rm xc}[n_\uparrow,n_\downarrow]\ge E_x[n_\uparrow,n_\downarrow]\ge -1.679\,e^2\int d^3r\, n^{4/3}$, that the exact functional respects and that I'd like mine to respect too — at least *locally*, point by point, which is the strong way to enforce it. For the spin-polarized enhancement, spin-scaling gives $F_x(\zeta=1,s)=2^{1/3}F_x(s/2^{1/3})$, so if $F_x(\zeta=1,s)$ grows gradually to a maximum value $\le 2.273$, the bound holds. That translates to $F_x(s)\le 1.804$: $2^{1/3}\times 1.804=2.2729\le 2.273$. So whatever $F_x(s)$ I write must *saturate*, not diverge — it has to level off at a ceiling $\le 1.804$ as $s\to\infty$. This immediately rules out a polynomial or any unbounded enhancement (which is part of why PW91's fit had to be contorted at large $s$). I need a bounded function with the right small-$s$ slope.

The simplest bounded enhancement with a tunable ceiling and a controlled small-$s$ slope is
$$F_x(s)=1+\kappa-\frac{\kappa}{1+\mu s^2/\kappa}.$$
Check it. $F_x(0)=1+\kappa-\kappa=1$ — uniform gas recovered. Small $s$: expand $\kappa/(1+\mu s^2/\kappa)\approx\kappa(1-\mu s^2/\kappa)=\kappa-\mu s^2$, so $F_x\approx 1+\kappa-(\kappa-\mu s^2)=1+\mu s^2$ — condition (f), with exactly the slope $\mu$ I chose. Large $s$: the fraction $\to 0$, so $F_x\to 1+\kappa$ — a hard ceiling. Set $1+\kappa=1.804$, i.e. $\kappa=0.804$, and the local Lieb–Oxford bound is saturated, condition (g). Two limits, two parameters, both fixed by exact conditions, no fitting. (Becke proposed exactly this functional form in 1986, but with $\kappa=0.967$, $\mu=0.235$ fit to atomic exchange energies — I'm using the same shape but fixing both coefficients non-empirically, $\kappa$ by the bound and $\mu$ by the linear-response cancellation.)

Why is the ceiling the *maximum* allowed value rather than something smaller? Because the enhancement should turn on exchange as strongly as it's allowed to as the gradient grows, and the bound is the only thing telling me where to stop. Choosing $\kappa$ at the bound is the non-empirical choice; any value below it would be an arbitrary fit. (There's a connection to the hole picture worth noting: a *sharp* real-space cutoff of the gradient-expanded exchange hole corresponds to exactly $\kappa=0.804$, while a more diffuse cutoff would give a smaller $\kappa$ — so saturating the bound is also the sharp-cutoff member of the family. The bound picks it out cleanly without my having to redo the cutoff.)

Let me step back and look at the combined object. To portray the nonlocality of the whole thing, define an enhancement factor over local exchange for the *full* exchange–correlation energy,
$$E_{\rm xc}^{\rm GGA}[n_\uparrow,n_\downarrow]=\int d^3r\; n\,\epsilon_x^{\rm unif}(n)\,F_{\rm xc}(r_s,\zeta,s).$$
This represents any GGA exactly when $\zeta$ is independent of $\mathbf r$, and is always approximately valid; LSD is the further approximation $F_{\rm xc}(r_s,\zeta,s)\to F_{\rm xc}(r_s,\zeta,0)$. The behavior is physically sensible: exchange dominates the high-density limit, $F_{\rm xc}\to F_x(\zeta,s)$ as $r_s\to 0$; and as $s$ increases at fixed $r_s$, exchange turns *on* more strongly (the enhancement climbs toward $1+\kappa$) while correlation turns *off* (the bracket $\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}+H\to 0$). Exchange nonlocality dominates for valence-electron densities $1\lesssim r_s/a_0\lesssim 10$, so for most physical densities the functional favors inhomogeneity more than LSD — exactly the corrective direction I wanted. Only in the low-density limit is correlation strong enough that its nonlocality dominates instead.

Let me be honest about what I gave up, because the design was a trade and I should own both sides. I sacrificed (1) the correct second-order gradient coefficients for $E_x$ and $E_c$ in the slowly-varying limit — that's the $\mu=0.2195$-not-$10/81$ decision, and the analogous correlation choice — and (2) the correct nonuniform scaling of $E_x$ in limits where $s\to\infty$. Both are genuine properties of the exact functional that PW91 kept and I deliberately dropped. The defense is the one I started from: real systems are never globally slowly varying and rarely probe $s\to\infty$ in an energetically important way, whereas they *do* probe linear response (simple metals) and *do* need a finite high-density correlation energy and a smooth potential. I spent the unimportant constraints to buy the important ones, and the reward is a functional whose every parameter beyond the uniform-gas inputs is a fundamental constant, whose derivation is three correlation conditions plus four exchange conditions instead of a real-space cutoff, and whose potential is smooth because nothing was contorted to fit a numerical curve.

So the method, assembled. Correlation:
$$E_c^{\rm GGA}=\int d^3r\; n\big[\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}(r_s,\zeta)+H(r_s,\zeta,t)\big],$$
$$H=(e^2/a_0)\,\gamma\,\phi^3\,\ln\!\Big\{1+\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\,t^2\,\frac{1+At^2}{1+At^2+A^2t^4}\Big\},\quad A=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}\Big[e^{-\epsilon_c^{\rm unif}/(\gamma\phi^3 e^2/a_0)}-1\Big]^{-1},$$
$\beta=0.066725$, $\gamma=(1-\ln2)/\pi^2=0.031091$, $t=|\nabla n|/(2\phi k_s n)$, $\phi=\tfrac12[(1+\zeta)^{2/3}+(1-\zeta)^{2/3}]$, $k_s=\sqrt{4k_F/\pi a_0}$. Exchange:
$$E_x^{\rm GGA}=\int d^3r\; n\,\epsilon_x^{\rm unif}(n)\,F_x(s),\quad F_x(s)=1+\kappa-\frac{\kappa}{1+\mu s^2/\kappa},$$
$\kappa=0.804$, $\mu=\beta\pi^2/3=0.21951$, $s=|\nabla n|/(2k_F n)$, $\epsilon_x^{\rm unif}=-3e^2k_F/4\pi$, with the polarized exchange from spin-scaling $E_x[n_\uparrow,n_\downarrow]=\tfrac12(E_x[2n_\uparrow]+E_x[2n_\downarrow])$.

Causal chain, top to bottom: the energy only sees integrated hole constraints, so I keep those and drop the rest; the semilocal form can't honor every exact condition, so I keep only the energetically significant ones — linear response in, slowly-varying coefficient out; correlation built from three limits (slowly-varying $\beta t^2$, rapidly-varying vanish, high-density log-cancellation) collapses into one $\ln(1+\dots)$ ansatz whose interpolation bracket and constant $A$ are fixed by those same limits; exchange built from four (uniform scaling fixing the $F_x(s)$ form, spin-scaling reducing to the unpolarized case, linear-response fixing $\mu=\beta\pi^2/3$, Lieb–Oxford fixing $\kappa=0.804$) collapses into the bounded $1+\kappa-\kappa/(1+\mu s^2/\kappa)$; every constant is fundamental; the potential is smooth because nothing was fit.

Here it is as a kernel — given the spin densities and gradients on a grid point, return the exchange–correlation energy density. Each block is one of the conditions above.

    A    = (BETA / GAMMA) / (np.expm1(-ec / (GAMMA * phi**3)))
    t2   = t * t
    frac = (1.0 + A*t2) / (1.0 + A*t2 + A*A*t2*t2)     # interpolation bracket
    arg  = 1.0 + (BETA / GAMMA) * t2 * frac
    return GAMMA * phi**3 * np.log(arg)

def xc_energy_density(n_up, n_dn, grad_n_up, grad_n_dn):
    """PBE exchange-correlation energy density at a grid point."""
    n     = n_up + n_dn
    zeta  = (n_up - n_dn) / n
    grad  = np.abs(grad_n_up + grad_n_dn)
    kF    = (3.0 * np.pi**2 * n) ** (1.0/3.0)
    phi   = 0.5 * ((1.0 + zeta)**(2.0/3.0) + (1.0 - zeta)**(2.0/3.0))
    ks    = np.sqrt(4.0 * kF / np.pi)
    rs    = (3.0 / (4.0 * np.pi * n)) ** (1.0/3.0)

    s     = grad / (2.0 * kF * n)            # Fermi-wavelength reduced gradient (exchange)
    t     = grad / (2.0 * phi * ks * n)      # screening-length reduced gradient (correlation)

    # exchange: spin-scaling E_x[n_up,n_dn] = (E_x[2 n_up] + E_x[2 n_dn]) / 2  (cond. e)
    ex = 0.5 * (2.0*n_up * eps_x_unif(2.0*n_up) * Fx_pbe(np.abs(2.0*grad_n_up)/(2.0*(3*np.pi**2*2*n_up)**(1/3)*2*n_up))
              + 2.0*n_dn * eps_x_unif(2.0*n_dn) * Fx_pbe(np.abs(2.0*grad_n_dn)/(2.0*(3*np.pi**2*2*n_dn)**(1/3)*2*n_dn)))

    # correlation: LSD plus gradient correction H
    ec = n * (eps_c_unif(rs, zeta) + H_corr(rs, zeta, t))

    return ex + ec
