The question Kadison and Singer asked in 1959 — does every pure state on the abelian von Neumann algebra $\mathbb{D}$ of bounded diagonal operators on $\ell_2$ extend *uniquely* to a pure state on $B(\ell_2)$? — has by now been ground down to something completely finite. Anderson's compactness reduction turned it into a statement about *paving* matrices: for every $\epsilon>0$ there should be an $r$, independent of the size $n$, so that every zero-diagonal self-adjoint $n\times n$ matrix $T$ admits coordinate projections $P_1,\dots,P_r$ summing to $I$ with $\|P_kTP_k\|\le\epsilon\|T\|$ for each $k$. Weaver then recast a projection-paving conjecture of Akemann and Anderson as a discrepancy statement $KS_2$: there are universal $\eta\ge2$, $\theta>0$ such that any $w_1,\dots,w_m\in\mathbb{C}^d$ with $\|w_i\|\le1$ and $\sum_i|\langle u,w_i\rangle|^2=\eta$ for every unit $u$ can be two-colored so that each color class satisfies $\sum_{i\in S_j}|\langle u,w_i\rangle|^2\le\eta-\theta$. Both reformulations, stripped down, ask the same thing: given a sum $\sum_i v_iv_i^*$ of rank-one positive semidefinite matrices resolving the identity out of small pieces, make a *combinatorial* choice — a subset, a partition, a signing — so that the resulting sum has small operator norm. The flexible version that contains all of them is: let $v_1,\dots,v_m\in\mathbb{C}^d$ be independent random vectors of finite support with $\sum_i\mathbb{E}\,v_iv_i^*=I$ and $\mathbb{E}\,\|v_i\|^2\le\epsilon$, and ask how small $\big\|\sum_iv_iv_i^*\big\|$ can be made over outcomes. A partition into $r$ parts is just the case where each $v_i$ chooses its part at random. What is demanded is a bound at a **dimension-free** scale, and — crucially — an **existence** guarantee: *some* outcome is good, however unlikely.

That last requirement is what kills the obvious tool. These are sums of independent random rank-one matrices, exactly the object matrix concentration was built for, and the Ahlswede-Winter / Rudelson / matrix-Chernoff machinery does apply: $\mathbb{E}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,e^{\theta\sum_iv_iv_i^*}$ factorizes well enough to give $\big\|\sum_iv_iv_i^*\big\|\le C(\epsilon)\log n$ with high probability. It fails twice over. The scale is wrong — $n$ is the dimension, and paving needs $r$ independent of the matrix size, so anything growing with $n$ is dead on arrival. And the *kind* of statement is wrong: the good outcome can be exponentially rare (a union of disjoint cliques has a good signing for only an exponentially small fraction of signings), and concentration describes the typical outcome, so it is precisely blind to a rare good one. The partial results obtained under stronger hypotheses — Bourgain-Tzafriri restricted invertibility and the various partial pavings — top out at constants of order $1/\log n$. The deterministic barrier method of Batson-Spielman-Srivastava, developed for graph sparsification, does reach a dimension-free condition number $\frac{d+1+2\sqrt d}{d+1-2\sqrt d}$ by adding rank-one terms one at a time under a potential $\Phi^u(A)=\mathrm{Tr}(uI-A)^{-1}$, but it controls a *reweighted* sub-sum $\sum_is_iv_iv_i^*$ with real weights $s_i\ge0$, not a subset or a partition, and its potential machinery is univariate. There is also a primitive case where everything already works: if the $v_i$ are i.i.d. and isotropic, $\mathbb{E}\,vv^*=cI$, Cauchy interlacing supplies common interlacings and the matrix determinant lemma gives $\mathbb{E}\,\chi[A+vv^*]=(1-cD)\chi[A]$ with $D=\frac{d}{dx}$, so iterating from $\chi=x^n$ produces a scaled associated Laguerre polynomial with classically known extreme roots. That case is *entirely univariate* — all covariances are multiples of the identity, so all the operators commute and act on a single polynomial. The real problem has general, non-commuting covariances $A_i=\mathbb{E}\,v_iv_i^*$, and the collapse to one variable is unavailable.

I propose to settle it by the method of **interlacing families of mixed characteristic polynomials**, controlled by a **multivariate barrier argument**. The first move is to stop averaging the norm and average the characteristic polynomial instead. The norm of a positive semidefinite matrix is its largest eigenvalue, hence the largest root of $\chi[M](x)=\det(xI-M)$; a first moment on the norm is useless because a max is not an average, but the coefficients of $\chi$ do average. Averaging alone proves nothing, and it is worth seeing exactly why, because the failure dictates the structure to demand: the average of $(x-1)(x-2)$ and $(x-3)(x-4)$ is $x^2-5x+7$, whose roots $2.5\pm\sqrt3\,i$ are not even real. Roots of an average are in general unrelated to roots of the summands. The classical condition under which they *are* related is interlacing. A real-rooted $g$ of degree $n-1$ interlaces a real-rooted $f$ of degree $n$ if their roots alternate, and $f_1,\dots,f_k$ have a *common interlacing* if one $g$ interlaces all of them. If they do, let $\alpha_{n-1}$ be the largest root of $g$: each $f_i$ has positive leading coefficient and exactly one root $\ge\alpha_{n-1}$, so $f_i(\alpha_{n-1})\le0$; hence $f_\emptyset=\sum_if_i$ is $\le0$ there and has a largest root $\beta_n\ge\alpha_{n-1}$; and since $\sum_if_i(\beta_n)=0$, some $f_i$ is $\ge0$ at $\beta_n$ while being $\le0$ at $\alpha_{n-1}$ with only one root past $\alpha_{n-1}$, so that $f_i$'s largest root lies in $[\alpha_{n-1},\beta_n]$. Some summand's largest root is at most the sum's.

One application is not enough, because the outcomes are indexed by a product set $S_1\times\cdots\times S_m$ and I cannot expect all leaves to share a single interlacer. So I organize them into an *interlacing family*: leaf polynomials $f_{s_1,\dots,s_m}$, real-rooted with positive leading coefficients, such that at every partial assignment $s_1,\dots,s_k$ the children $\{f_{s_1,\dots,s_k,t}\}_t$ over the next coordinate have a common interlacing, where an internal node is the sum of its subtree. Walking down the tree and applying the lemma at each level gives the principle I need: an interlacing family always contains a leaf whose largest root is at most the largest root of $f_\emptyset$. Taking the leaves to be the per-outcome characteristic polynomials weighted by their probabilities, $f_\emptyset$ is the expected characteristic polynomial, and the whole problem collapses to two tasks: prove the family is interlacing, and bound the largest root of that one expected polynomial.

The expected characteristic polynomial has a closed form, and finding it is where the non-commuting covariances are handled — not by fusing them into one operator, but by giving each its own variable. For a single random vector and any square matrix $M$, the matrix determinant lemma gives $\mathbb{E}\,\det(M-vv^*)=\det(M)-\det(M)\,\mathrm{Tr}(M^{-1}\mathbb{E}\,vv^*)$ for invertible $M$, while Jacobi's formula gives exactly the same value for $(1-\partial_t)\det(M+t\,\mathbb{E}\,vv^*)|_{t=0}$; both sides are polynomial in the entries of $M$, so the identity extends by continuity. A random rank-one update *is* the operator $1-\partial_t$ acting on a determinant that carries the covariance on a fresh variable. Peeling off one vector at a time and using independence and linearity to move each $1-\partial_{z_i}$ and its evaluation outside the remaining expectation, induction on $k$ gives $\mathbb{E}\,\det(M-\sum_{i\le k}v_iv_i^*)=\big(\prod_{i\le k}(1-\partial_{z_i})\big)\det(M+\sum_{i\le k}z_iA_i)|_{z=0}$, and setting $M=xI$, $k=m$ yields
$$\mathbb{E}\,\chi\Big[\sum_{i=1}^mv_iv_i^*\Big](x)=\Big(\prod_{i=1}^m(1-\partial_{z_i})\Big)\det\Big(xI+\sum_{i=1}^mz_iA_i\Big)\Big|_{z=0}=:\mu[A_1,\dots,A_m](x),$$
which I call the **mixed characteristic polynomial** of $A_1,\dots,A_m$ — mixed because for $m=d$ its constant coefficient is the mixed discriminant $\big(\prod_i\partial_{z_i}\big)\det(\sum_iz_iA_i)$ from Gurvits's capacity work. It depends only on the covariances. Each $A_i$ rides its own variable, so the covariances never have to be reconciled with one another; they are held apart and only fused at the end by $z_i\mapsto0$.

Keeping the variables apart is also what delivers real-rootedness, through the multivariate cousin of it. A polynomial $p\in\mathbb{C}[z_1,\dots,z_m]$ is *real stable* if it has real coefficients and never vanishes when every $z_i$ has strictly positive imaginary part; in one variable real stable means real-rooted. Three facts close the loop. The seed $\det(xI+\sum_iz_iA_i)$ is real stable for positive semidefinite $A_i$, because with all imaginary parts positive $\mathrm{Im}(xI+\sum_iz_iA_i)\succ0$ and the matrix is nonsingular. Each $1-\partial_{z_i}$ preserves real stability: fixing the other variables to points with positive imaginary part, the univariate restriction factors as $q(z)=c\prod_j(z-w_j)$ with all $\mathrm{Im}\,w_j\le0$, and $q-q'=q\big(1-\sum_j\frac1{z-w_j}\big)$ cannot vanish for $\mathrm{Im}\,z>0$ since each $\frac1{z-w_j}$ then has negative imaginary part. And setting a variable to a real number preserves it. So $\mu[A_1,\dots,A_m](x)$ is real stable in one variable, i.e. real-rooted, whenever the $A_i$ are positive semidefinite. That is exactly the hypothesis the interlacing-family property needs, via the characterization of Dedieu, Fell and Chudnovsky-Seymour: equal-degree polynomials with positive leading coefficients have a common interlacing **iff** every convex combination of them is real-rooted. At a node with $j_1,\dots,j_k$ fixed, a convex combination $\sum_t\lambda_tq_{j_1,\dots,j_k,t}$ of the children over coordinate $k+1$ is, up to the fixed constant $\prod_{i\le k}p_{i,j_i}$, the mixed characteristic polynomial one gets by letting coordinate $k+1$ be a fresh random vector taking $w_{k+1,t}$ with probability $\lambda_t$ — real-rooted by the above. Every convex combination is real-rooted, so every node's children have a common interlacing, and the outcome polynomials form an interlacing family. Real stability is needed for exactly this, and for the barrier estimates below.

What remains is quantitative: bound the largest root of $\mu[A_1,\dots,A_m]$ when $\sum_iA_i=I$ and $\mathrm{Tr}(A_i)\le\epsilon$. Substituting $y_i=z_i+x$ and using $\sum_ixA_i=xI$ rewrites it as $\mu(x)=Q(x,\dots,x)$ with $Q(y)=\big(\prod_i(1-\partial_{y_i})\big)P(y)$ and seed $P(y)=\det(\sum_iy_iA_i)$, so the object is a clean multivariate polynomial built from a determinant by one operator per coordinate, and the task is to push a point up the diagonal until it is past all the roots of $Q$. Call $z\in\mathbb{R}^m$ *above the roots* of $p$ if $p(z+t)>0$ for every coordinatewise nonnegative $t$; if $(1+\sqrt\epsilon)^2\mathbf1$ is above the roots of $Q$ then $\mu(x)>0$ for all $x\ge(1+\sqrt\epsilon)^2$ and the largest root is at most that. The control device is a barrier function, but *vector-valued* — one per direction, which is the point of departure from the univariate potential:
$$\Phi^i_p(z)=\frac{\partial_{z_i}p(z)}{p(z)}=\partial_{z_i}\log p(z)=\sum_j\frac1{z_i-\lambda_j},$$
the $\lambda_j$ being the roots of the univariate restriction in coordinate $i$ — the same Stieltjes-type sum as the sparsification potential, one copy per direction, blowing up as coordinate $i$ descends toward a root.

Two analytic properties carry the induction: above the roots, $\partial_{z_j}\Phi^i_p\le0$ and $\partial^2_{z_j}\Phi^i_p\ge0$ for *all* $i,j$. Along $i=j$ this is immediate from $\partial^2_{z_i}\frac1{z_i-\lambda_j}=\frac2{(z_i-\lambda_j)^3}>0$ with $z_i>\lambda_j$. The cross direction $i\ne j$ is where real-rootedness alone would leave me stuck, since a univariate restriction says nothing across two variables, and this is where the bivariate structure must be used: by Helton-Vinnikov (with the Lax-conjecture representation of Lewis-Parrilo-Ramana) the bivariate restriction is $\det(z_iB_i+z_jB_j+C)$ with $B_i,B_j\succeq0$ and $C$ Hermitian, with $M=z_iB_i+z_jB_j+C\succ0$ above the roots, so Jacobi turns the barrier into a trace, $\Phi^i_p=\mathrm{Tr}(M^{-1}B_i)$, and expanding $(M+\delta B_j)^{-1}$ reads off
$$\partial_{z_j}\Phi^i_p=-\mathrm{Tr}\big(B_jM^{-1}B_iM^{-1}\big)\le0,\qquad \partial^2_{z_j}\Phi^i_p=\mathrm{Tr}\big((B_jM^{-1}B_j)(M^{-1}B_iM^{-1})\big)\ge0,$$
both being traces of products of positive semidefinite matrices. This determinantal representation is the one heavy ingredient; more elementary routes to the same monotonicity and convexity exist, but this one makes the trace identities transparent, so I keep it.

Monotonicity alone already gives the crude step: if $\Phi^i_p(z)<1$ then $z$ stays above the roots of $p-\partial_{z_i}p$, since $\Phi^i_p(z+t)<1$ means $\partial_{z_i}p(z+t)<p(z+t)$ for all $t\ge0$. But that does not survive $m$ iterations — each operator can push the barriers up, and after a few steps they can pass $1$ and the argument dies. The fix is to keep the barriers bounded *away* from $1$ and pay for each operator with a fixed shift, using convexity to make the shift cancel the barrier's tendency to rise. Concretely: if $z$ is above the roots of $p$ and $\Phi^j_p(z)\le1-\frac1\delta$, then $\Phi^i_{p-\partial_{z_j}p}(z+\delta e_j)\le\Phi^i_p(z)$ for every $i$. This follows from the exact identity $\Phi^i_{p-\partial_jp}=\Phi^i_p-\frac{\partial_j\Phi^i_p}{1-\Phi^j_p}$ (using $\partial_i\Phi^j_p=\partial_i\partial_j\log p=\partial_j\Phi^i_p$): the claim becomes $-\frac{\partial_j\Phi^i_p(z+\delta e_j)}{1-\Phi^j_p(z+\delta e_j)}\le\Phi^i_p(z)-\Phi^i_p(z+\delta e_j)$, and convexity in direction $j$ bounds the right side below by $\delta\cdot(-\partial_j\Phi^i_p(z+\delta e_j))$; dividing by $-\partial_j\Phi^i_p\ge0$ (the zero case being trivial) reduces everything to $\frac1{1-\Phi^j_p(z+\delta e_j)}\le\delta$, which monotonicity in $j$ and the hypothesis $\Phi^j_p(z)\le1-\frac1\delta$ supply. So applying $1-\partial_{y_j}$ and stepping coordinate $j$ up by $\delta$ leaves every barrier no larger than before: they never accumulate.

Now the numbers. Since $\sum_iA_i=I$, $\det(\sum_itA_i)=t^d>0$, so $t\mathbf1$ is above the roots of $P$ for every $t>0$, and Jacobi gives $\Phi^i_P(t\mathbf1)=\mathrm{Tr}((tI)^{-1}A_i)=\mathrm{Tr}(A_i)/t\le\epsilon/t=:\phi$. Setting $\delta=\frac1{1-\phi}$ makes the hypothesis of the shift lemma hold exactly. With $P_k=\big(\prod_{i\le k}(1-\partial_{y_i})\big)P$ and $x^k$ the point equal to $t+\delta$ in the first $k$ coordinates and $t$ elsewhere, induction gives that $x^k$ is above the roots of $P_k$ with every $\Phi^i_{P_k}(x^k)\le\phi$; after $m$ steps $(t+\delta)\mathbf1$ is above the roots of $Q=P_m$, so the largest root of $\mu$ is at most $t+\delta$. Minimizing $t+\frac{t}{t-\epsilon}$ over $t>\epsilon$ gives $(t-\epsilon)^2=\epsilon$, i.e. $t=\sqrt\epsilon+\epsilon$; then $\phi=\frac{\sqrt\epsilon}{1+\sqrt\epsilon}$, $\delta=1+\sqrt\epsilon$, and
$$t+\delta=(\sqrt\epsilon+\epsilon)+(1+\sqrt\epsilon)=(1+\sqrt\epsilon)^2.$$
The constant is not arbitrary: it has the shape of the associated-Laguerre and Heilmann-Lieb extreme-root bounds against which the isotropic and regular cases calibrate, so it is the right tight scale.

Every design choice in this is forced. Characteristic polynomials rather than matrices, because polynomials satisfy the multilinear identities that make averages computable and admit real-stability and interlacing tools, while concentration on matrices only reaches the wrong $\log n$ scale. The *expected* characteristic polynomial — a first moment — rather than moments or Stieltjes transforms, because existence at nonzero probability is what is required and a root of the average can dominate a root of some summand, which no concentration statement can certify for a rare outcome. Interlacing families, because roots of a generic average are unrelated to those of the summands and common interlacing is precisely the structure under which "some summand's largest root is at most the average's" holds, lifted up the product tree. The product $\prod_i(1-\partial_{z_i})$ on $\det(xI+\sum_iz_iA_i)$, because a random rank-one update *is* that operator, and one variable per non-commuting covariance is what lets multivariate real-stability theory apply where the univariate $(1-cD)$ picture, valid only for identically distributed isotropic vectors, fails. A vector-valued barrier with a $\delta$-shift, because $\Phi^i<1$ alone keeps a point above the roots for one operator but lets the barriers creep upward, whereas bounding $\Phi^j$ below $1$ by $1/\delta$ and stepping coordinate $j$ by $\delta$ uses convexity to keep every barrier non-increasing, so an $m$-step induction closes.

Assembling: $A_i=\mathbb{E}\,v_iv_i^*$ satisfies $\mathrm{Tr}(A_i)=\mathbb{E}\,\|v_i\|^2\le\epsilon$ and $\sum_iA_i=I$; the expected characteristic polynomial is $\mu[A_1,\dots,A_m]$, whose largest root is at most $(1+\sqrt\epsilon)^2$; the outcome polynomials form an interlacing family, so some outcome $\sum_iw_{i,j_i}w_{i,j_i}^*$ has largest characteristic root — that is, operator norm — at most $(1+\sqrt\epsilon)^2$, and that outcome has positive probability.

**Theorem.** Let $v_1,\dots,v_m$ be independent random vectors in $\mathbb{C}^d$ with finite support such that $\sum_{i=1}^m\mathbb{E}\,v_iv_i^*=I_d$ and $\mathbb{E}\,\|v_i\|^2\le\epsilon$ for all $i$. Then
$$\Pr\Big[\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^mv_iv_i^*\Big\|\le(1+\sqrt\epsilon)^2\Big]>0.$$

**Corollary (partition).** If $u_1,\dots,u_m\in\mathbb{C}^d$ satisfy $\sum_iu_iu_i^*=I$ and $\|u_i\|^2\le\delta$, then for every integer $r$ there is a partition $S_1,\dots,S_r$ of $[m]$ with
$$\Big\|\sum_{i\in S_k}u_iu_i^*\Big\|\le\Big(\frac1{\sqrt r}+\sqrt\delta\Big)^2\quad\text{for every }k.$$
To see it, let $w_{i,k}\in\mathbb{C}^{rd}$ be $u_i$ placed in the $k$-th block and zero elsewhere, and let $v_i$ take the value $\sqrt r\,w_{i,k}$ with probability $1/r$. Then $\sum_i\mathbb{E}\,v_iv_i^*=I_{rd}$ and $\|v_i\|^2=r\|u_i\|^2\le r\delta$, so the theorem with $\epsilon=r\delta$ gives an assignment — that is, a partition $S_k=\{i:v_i=\sqrt r\,w_{i,k}\}$ — whose block-diagonal norm is at most $(1+\sqrt{r\delta})^2$; the $k$-th block contributes $r\big\|\sum_{i\in S_k}u_iu_i^*\big\|$, and dividing by $r$ gives the stated bound.

From this the universal constants fall out. Taking $r=2$ and $\delta=1/18$, each part satisfies $\big(1/\sqrt2+1/(3\sqrt2)\big)^2=16/18=8/9<1$. In Weaver's normalization $u_i=w_i/\sqrt\eta$ with $\eta=18$, so $\delta=1/\eta$, multiplying back by $\eta=18$ turns $8/9$ into $16=\eta-\theta$: $KS_2$ holds with $\eta=18$, $\theta=2$. Since $KS_2$ implies Akemann-Anderson projection paving, which implies Kadison-Singer, **the Kadison-Singer problem has a positive answer**: every pure state on the diagonal algebra extends uniquely.

The paving constant comes out explicitly as well. By the Casazza-Edidin-Kalra-Paulsen reduction it suffices to $(r,\frac{1+\epsilon}2)$-pave every $2n\times2n$ projection $Q$ with diagonal entries $1/2$; such a $Q$ is the Gram matrix of vectors $u_i$ with $\|u_i\|^2=1/2$ resolving the identity, so the partition corollary with $\delta=1/2$ gives $\|P_kQP_k\|\le(1/\sqrt r+1/\sqrt2)^2<1/2+3/\sqrt r\le\frac{1+\epsilon}2$ for $r=36/\epsilon^2$. Propagating that through the reduction yields the final statement: for every $\epsilon>0$, every zero-diagonal complex self-adjoint matrix is $(r,\epsilon)$-pavable with
$$r=(6/\epsilon)^4,$$
a constant independent of the size of the matrix — against the known floor of $r\approx1/\epsilon^2$, the dimension-free paving constant the whole edifice was demanding.
