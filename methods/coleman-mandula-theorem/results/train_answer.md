The proton and the neutron sit in an isospin doublet, related by an internal $SU(2)$ whose generators commute with the four-momentum, and that commutation is precisely what forces $P_\mu P^\mu$ to take the same eigenvalue $m^2$ on both members: the symmetry that relates them also pins them to a common mass, which is not what the spectrum shows. The tempting way out is to let an internal generator fail to commute with $P_\mu$ — with, say, $[P_\mu, I_+] = c\, I_+$ one rearranges to $m_p^2 = m_n^2 - c^2$, and the splitting the commuting symmetry forbids becomes a number to be read off the data. The same hope appears one level up. Gürsey and Radicati, and Sakita, glued the rest-frame spin $SU(2)$ onto the Gell-Mann–Ne'eman flavour $SU(3)$ to form $SU(6)$, in which the pseudoscalar and vector mesons combine into a single $\mathbf{35}$ and the spin-$\tfrac12$ octet and spin-$\tfrac32$ decuplet into a single $\mathbf{56}$. That already mixes an internal quantum number with a spacetime one, since spin says how a state transforms under rotations — but only non-relativistically, because the rotations used are rest-frame rotations rather than the full Lorentz group. Every attempt to complete $SU(6)$ into a relativistic group containing the whole Poincaré group produced untenable spectra and untenable scattering. The question is whether that is a run of clumsy constructions or a wall.

The no-go theorems that already exist do not settle it. McGlinn assumes at the outset that the combined algebra is spanned by the Poincaré basis together with the internal basis, and that $[I, M] = 0$; from those he derives $[I, P] = 0$ and a direct product. But "the basis of $T$ is the basis of $L$ union the basis of $I$" forbids by hypothesis exactly the objects at issue — generators that are neither Poincaré nor internal but genuinely interpolate. O'Raifeartaigh does far better: he drops the basis assumption, embeds the Poincaré algebra into an arbitrary finite-order Lie algebra, and lets the Levi decomposition $T = S \rtimes G$ into radical and semisimple factor enumerate the cases; in every case $M \cap S = 0$, and the physically admissible case returns $T = L \times I$, so all particles in a multiplet of the combined group are mass-degenerate. Yet the Levi decomposition needs finite order, so the infinite-parameter supermultiplet constructions, with infinitely many particle types spread along the mass spectrum, simply escape it; Michel's reframing of the Poincaré group as a quotient $E/S$ sharpens the hypotheses but stays in the same arena. Above all, these arguments live entirely on the one-particle spectrum. They never ask what happens when two particles actually collide — and the decisive clue is that the loophole groups which survive the group theory give trivial scattering, amplitudes vanishing except in the forward and backward directions. What the abstract arguments cannot see is exactly what kills their loopholes.

So I propose the Coleman–Mandula theorem, and with it a change of arena: stop asking which abstract group can contain the Poincaré group, and ask which symmetry can commute with an honest, non-trivial, analytic $S$-matrix. Then nothing need be assumed about normality of the translations or the dimension of the group — scattering is the judge, and multiparticle information, the missing ingredient, is used from the start. A symmetry is a unitary operator $U$ that maps one-particle states to one-particle states, acts on many-particle states as if they were tensor products — so its infinitesimal generators are *additive*, the generator on $|p,q\rangle$ being the generator on $p$ plus the generator on $q$ — and commutes with $S$. The hypotheses that carry the proof are Lorentz invariance (the symmetry group contains a subgroup locally isomorphic to Poincaré), particle-finiteness (all particle types are positive-energy Poincaré representations and only finitely many lie below any finite mass), weak elastic analyticity (elastic amplitudes are analytic in $s$ and $t$ near the physical region, away from normal thresholds), the occurrence of scattering (any two one-particle momentum eigenstates scatter into something except perhaps at isolated $s$, so $S \neq 1$), and one technical assumption, that the generators are integral operators in momentum space with distribution kernels.

The physics is visible before any rigor. Suppose there were a conserved symmetric traceless tensor charge $Q_{\mu\nu}$, genuinely new — not $J_{\mu\nu}$ and not $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ times something. Lorentz covariance forces its one-particle expectation value to be built from $p$ alone, $\langle p|Q_{\mu\nu}|p\rangle \propto p_\mu p_\nu - \tfrac14 \eta_{\mu\nu} p^2$, and additivity gives $(p_1)_\mu (p_1)_\nu + (p_2)_\mu (p_2)_\nu$ on a two-particle state, with the trace pieces fixed by the masses. In an elastic collision $p_1, p_2 \to q_1, q_2$ the four-momentum law lets me write $q_1 = p_1 + a$, $q_2 = p_2 - a$, and substituting that into the conservation of $Q$ cancels the quadratic pieces and leaves
$$a_\mu (p_1 - p_2)_\nu + a_\nu (p_1 - p_2)_\mu + 2 a_\mu a_\nu = 0,$$
whose only solution at generic incoming momenta is $a_\mu = 0$: the particles pass straight through. A conserved charge carrying uncontracted Lorentz indices imposes conservation laws *on top of* energy–momentum conservation and so over-determines the kinematics, freezing elastic scattering to isolated configurations. Analyticity then finishes the job, because an amplitude analytic in the scattering angle and non-zero only at isolated points vanishes identically — contradicting the premise that scattering occurs. Both extra hypotheses are load-bearing and neither is decorative: without analyticity the isolated angles survive as a genuine loophole, and without non-triviality the theorem is vacuous, since when $S = 1$ every unitary operator is a symmetry. The expected conclusion is already legible: the only conserved generators carrying Lorentz indices are $P_\mu$ and $J_{\mu\nu}$ themselves, and everything else is a Lorentz scalar, i.e. internal.

Making that rigorous means allowing a generator to do much more than multiply by a function of $p$ — it may shuffle spin and type indices, connect different momenta, or act as a high-order differential operator. I split the generators into those that commute with $P_\mu$, called $\mathfrak B$, and those that do not, called $\mathfrak A$. A $B_\alpha \in \mathfrak B$ cannot move momentum, so on one-particle states it is a matrix $b_\alpha(p)$ on the discrete indices — finite by particle-finiteness, Hermitian by unitarity — and additivity gives $b_\alpha(p,q) = b_\alpha(p) \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes b_\alpha(q)$. The $b_\alpha(p)$ inherit the structure constants, so $B_\alpha \mapsto b_\alpha(p)$ is a homomorphism; what I need is that it is an *isomorphism*, because the theorem that a Lie algebra of finite Hermitian matrices is a compact semisimple algebra direct-sum some $u(1)$'s applies to the matrices, not to the abstract algebra. Scattering supplies the injectivity. Sandwiching $[B_\alpha, S] = 0$ between elastic two-particle states gives $b_\alpha(p',q')\,M = M\,b_\alpha(p,q)$ with $M$ the amplitude, invertible at almost all momenta by analyticity and non-triviality, hence
$$b_\alpha(p',q') = M\,b_\alpha(p,q)\,M^{-1},$$
so the two-particle representations at momenta connected by a collision are similarity-related and any linear dependence $c^\alpha b_\alpha(p,q) = 0$ propagates. Feeding that back through the tensor structure gives $c^\alpha b_\alpha(p') \otimes 1 = -\,1 \otimes c^\alpha b_\alpha(q')$, which forces each side to be a multiple of the identity — not zero, so a residual piece survives. That residue is the trace, and the trace is where the argument turns: $\mathrm{Tr}\,b_\alpha(p,q) = N(m_q)\,\mathrm{tr}\,b_\alpha(p) + N(m_p)\,\mathrm{tr}\,b_\alpha(q)$ with $N(m)$ the finite multiplicity at mass $m$, and traces are similarity-invariant, so $\mathrm{tr}\,b_\alpha(p)/N(m_p)$ is an additive conserved charge and therefore linear in the momentum, $\mathrm{tr}\,b_\alpha(p)/N(m_p) = a_\alpha^\mu p_\mu$, with no constant term (a constant fails to balance in processes with different particle numbers on the two sides). The offending identity piece is thus a four-momentum charge in disguise, so I subtract it: $B^\#_\alpha \equiv B_\alpha - a_\alpha^\mu P_\mu$ still commutes with $P_\mu$ and has traceless $b^\#_\alpha(p)$. Now the chain closes, because a traceless multiple of the identity is zero, and $P_\mu$ itself is correctly pushed out of the would-be internal algebra by the same subtraction.

Vanishing at the four legs of one collision is still not vanishing everywhere, and the step that removes momentum conservation is the cleverest piece of kinematics in the argument: having obtained $c^\alpha b^\#_\alpha(p) = c^\alpha b^\#_\alpha(q') = 0$ from the collision $p,q \to p',q'$, I consider the *different* collision $p, q' \to k, (p + q' - k)$, whose total momentum $p + q'$ is not $p+q$; the elastic conditions leave enough freedom in the choice of $q'$ that the outgoing three-momentum $\mathbf k$ is unconstrained, and the similarity transform along this second collision gives $c^\alpha b^\#_\alpha(k) = 0$ for almost all on-shell $k$. The two remaining gaps close by the same tools: isolated bad momenta cannot survive, since a non-vanishing $c^\alpha b^\#_\alpha(k_*)$ would forbid the scattering of $k_*$ against almost everything while amplitudes remain analytic and non-zero nearby, and other mass shells are reached through inelastic processes, of which particle-finiteness guarantees there are only finitely many to reach. Hence $c^\alpha B^\#_\alpha = 0$ and the map is an isomorphism. Two consequences follow at once. Because the independent $b^\#_\alpha(p,q)$ are matrices on a space of dimension $N(m_p)N(m_q)$, there are finitely many of them, so $\mathfrak B^\#$ is finite-dimensional — never assumed, but forced by the $S$-matrix, which is exactly what shuts the infinite-parameter escape route. And the finite-Hermitian-matrix theorem now applies to $\mathfrak B^\#$ itself, decomposing it into a compact semisimple algebra plus $u(1)$'s.

It remains to show that $\mathfrak B^\#$ commutes with $J_{\mu\nu}$ as well as with $P_\mu$. For a $u(1)$ generator $B^\#_i$, the Jacobi identity together with $[P_\mu, B^\#_i] = 0$ and the Poincaré relation $[J, P_\mu] \sim P$ gives $[P_\mu,[J,B^\#_i]] = 0$, so $[J, B^\#_i]$ lies in $\mathfrak B^\#$; centrality of $B^\#_i$ then makes the double commutator $[B^\#_i,[J,B^\#_i]]$ vanish, and taking its expectation value in a two-particle state with $J$ a rotation diagonal with eigenvalue $\sigma$ yields
$$0 = 2\sum_{m'n'}\big(\sigma(m',n') - \sigma(m,n)\big)\,\big|(b^\#_i(p,q))_{m'n',mn}\big|^2 ,$$
a sum of non-negative terms, so every matrix element connecting different $J$-eigenvalues vanishes and $b^\#_i$ commutes with $J$. The semisimple part yields to a sharper argument. Conjugation by a Lorentz transformation preserves $\mathfrak B^\#$, so $U(\Lambda)\,B_a\,U(\Lambda)^{-1} = D(\Lambda)_a{}^b\,B_b$ for some finite-dimensional representation $D$ of the Lorentz group; Hermiticity of the $B_a$ makes $D$ unitary, and the Lorentz group is non-compact, so it has no non-trivial finite-dimensional unitary representation and $D(\Lambda) = 1$. That is, in the end, the crisp reason spacetime cannot rotate internal charges: a Lorentz-covariant family of Hermitian charges would have to be a unitary finite-dimensional Lorentz multiplet, and the only one is the singlet.

For $\mathfrak A$ I first show the kernel $A_\alpha(p',p)$ — which must vanish unless both momenta are on shell, since a symmetry cannot take a physical state off shell — is supported at $p' = p$. Suppose otherwise, and smear, $A_\alpha^f = \int d^4x\, e^{iP\cdot x} A_\alpha e^{-iP\cdot x} f(x)$ with $\tilde f$ concentrated near some $\Delta \neq 0$, which is again a symmetry generator and shifts momentum by about $\Delta$. In a generic collision $p_1,q_1 \to p_2,q_2$ the shifted momenta $q_1 + \Delta$, $p_2 + \Delta$, $q_2 + \Delta$ all leave the mass shell, so $A^f_\alpha$ annihilates three legs but not the first, and $[A^f_\alpha, S] = 0$ then forces the amplitude for generic scattering to vanish — a contradiction. A distribution supported at a point is a finite sum of derivatives of $\delta^4(p'-p)$, of some finite order $D_\alpha$, and this is where the distributional assumption earns its keep, since it is what bounds that order; so each $A_\alpha$ is at most a $D_\alpha$-th order differential operator in $p$. Taking $D_\alpha$ commutators, $B_\alpha^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_D} = [P^{\mu_1},[\cdots[P^{\mu_D},A_\alpha]\cdots]]$, lands back in $\mathfrak B$: each commutator with $P$ pulls out a factor $(p'-p)$, and $D+1$ such factors against at most $D$ derivatives of the delta vanish once $p'=p$. So $b_\alpha^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_D}(p) = (b^\#_\alpha)^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_D} + a_\alpha^{\mu\mu_1\cdots\mu_D}p_\mu$, symmetric in $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_D$ because the $P$'s commute among themselves. The mass-shell condition $[P^2, A_\alpha] = 0$ gives $p_{\mu_1}b_\alpha^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_D}(p) = 0$ for $D \geq 1$, and matching powers of $p$ kills the internal piece, $(b^\#_\alpha)^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_D} = 0$, while forcing antisymmetry in the first two indices, $a_\alpha^{\mu\mu_1\cdots} = -a_\alpha^{\mu_1\mu\cdots}$. A tensor antisymmetric in its first two indices and symmetric in its last $D$ cannot survive $D \geq 2$: chasing the indices,
$$a^{\mu\mu_1\mu_2\cdots} = -a^{\mu_1\mu\mu_2\cdots} = -a^{\mu_1\mu_2\mu\cdots} = +a^{\mu_2\mu_1\mu\cdots} = +a^{\mu_2\mu\mu_1\cdots} = -a^{\mu\mu_2\mu_1\cdots} = -a^{\mu\mu_1\mu_2\cdots},$$
so $a = -a = 0$ — the rigorous form of "no higher-rank tensor charges", matching the over-constraint cartoon. The case $D = 0$ returns a generator that commutes with $P_\mu$ and so was never in $\mathfrak A$. Only $D = 1$ survives, with $a_\alpha^{\mu\nu} = -a_\alpha^{\nu\mu}$ and $[P^\mu, A_\alpha] = a_\alpha^{\mu\nu}P_\nu$; comparing with $[P^\mu, J^{\rho\sigma}] = i(\eta^{\mu\sigma}P^\rho - \eta^{\mu\rho}P^\sigma)$ shows $A_\alpha + \tfrac{i}{2}a_\alpha^{\rho\sigma}J_{\rho\sigma}$ commutes with $P^\mu$, so
$$A_\alpha = -\tfrac{i}{2}\,a_\alpha^{\mu\nu}J_{\mu\nu} + B_\alpha ,$$
a Lorentz generator dressed harmlessly with an internal piece. Every symmetry generator is therefore a translation, a Lorentz transformation, or an internal symmetry.

It is worth being explicit about where the argument leans, because the leaning points are the loopholes. It uses massive particles crucially, through $[P^2, A_\alpha] = 0$ and the power-matching that produced the antisymmetry; for purely massless particles $p^2 = 0$ and that matching collapses, so generators of order $D \geq 2$ are no longer forced to vanish — which is why the dilatation and special conformal generators of a massless theory may mix with Poincaré, as the conclusion correctly does not forbid. It uses genuine angular scattering, so in $1+1$ dimensions, where only forward and backward directions exist and the scattering angle is not an analytic variable, the analyticity hypothesis is empty and integrable models may carry infinitely many higher conserved charges. It uses the $S$-matrix as the arena, so symmetries that do not act on $S$ in the required way — spontaneously broken symmetries, discrete symmetries — are outside its scope. And every step manipulates *commutators* of bosonic Hermitian charges forming a Lie algebra: the additivity, the finite-Hermitian-matrix decomposition, the Lorentz-representation argument all rest on the Lie bracket. Charges that close under *anticommutators* — spinorial generators of a graded algebra — are not even statements in this proof and are therefore not excluded. That is the one door left open.

The result, stated as it should be stated: let $G$ be a connected symmetry group of the $S$-matrix, that is, a group of unitary operators which (a) map one-particle states to one-particle states, (b) act on many-particle states as tensor products, so that the generators are additive, and (c) commute with $S$; and assume (i) $G$ contains a subgroup locally isomorphic to the Poincaré group, (ii) all particle types are positive-energy Poincaré representations with only finitely many below any finite mass, (iii) elastic amplitudes are analytic in $s$ and $t$ in a neighbourhood of the physical region away from normal thresholds, (iv) any two one-particle momentum eigenstates scatter into something except perhaps at isolated values of $s$, and (v) the generators, as integral operators in momentum space, have distribution kernels. Then $G$ is locally isomorphic to the direct product of the Poincaré group with an internal symmetry group, and when the particle types are finite in number that internal group has compact closure:
$$
\boxed{\;\mathfrak g \;=\; \mathcal P \,\oplus\, \mathfrak g_{\rm internal},\qquad [\,\mathcal P,\ \mathfrak g_{\rm internal}\,]=0,\quad \mathfrak g_{\rm internal}\ \text{compact}.\;}
$$
Spacetime and internal symmetries cannot mix non-trivially: the mass-splitting hope and the relativistic $SU(6)$ hope die together, because any generator able to relate different masses or different spins would carry uncontracted Lorentz indices, would over-determine elastic kinematics, and analyticity would then force the scattering to vanish — contradicting the bare fact that particles scatter.
