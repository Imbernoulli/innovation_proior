The thing I need to expose is not whether the network can fit the data. It can. The random-label evidence already tells me that a sufficiently large neural network can store arbitrary finite labels, and the small transformer I plan to use will have far more degrees of freedom than a small symbolic dataset needs. The hidden variable is which solution the optimizer is moving toward while the training set is already fit: a table-lookup solution that only stores observed facts, or a compact rule-like solution that also answers the unobserved cases.

So I need a setting where those two solutions make different predictions and where I can measure that difference exactly. Natural data is too soft for this. If I train on images or text, I do not know the exact rule, and I can always argue about distribution shift or semantic similarity. I want a finite world where every possible query has a known answer. A mathematical operation gives me that immediately: for each pair of elements, there is one result. If I reveal some entries and hide the rest, train accuracy measures whether the model remembers the revealed entries, while validation accuracy measures whether it has inferred the missing entries of the same object.

Now I have to be careful not to smuggle the rule into the input representation. If I write numbers as decimal digits, the model can use digit algorithms. If I write permutations in line notation, the notation itself exposes structure. That contaminates the experiment because a held-out answer might come from parsing the representation rather than learning the operation from examples. I therefore make every element an opaque token. The model sees one symbol for each residue or permutation, plus operation and equals tokens. It receives no internal decomposition of the element. The only path to a held-out cell is through the pattern of interactions among symbols across the equations it has seen.

The dataset becomes a full collection of token sequences of the form `<a> <op> <b> <=> <c>`. I take a random fraction of all sequences as training examples and keep the rest as validation. This split is important: train and validation are not different kinds of data, they are disjoint cells of the same table. I score only the right-hand side after the equals sign, because the prefix is the query and the answer side is what the model has to infer. For the binary-operation rows this means the answer symbol, and in a shifted sequence implementation it can also include the trailing end token. A small decoder-only transformer is enough; I am not trying to win an architecture contest. I want a standard overparameterized learner that I can train cheaply for many more steps than ordinary practice would tolerate.

I expect the first phase to be memorization. The model can see a finite set of short sequences, and nothing prevents it from fitting them directly. My prediction for a case such as modular division with half the table revealed: training accuracy should climb to near-perfect well before the run has used much of its total optimization budget, while validation accuracy stays at chance over that same early window. Read on its own, a snapshot taken there would look like ordinary overfitting — the model fits what it has seen and shows no sign of the rule. Whether that read is the right one is exactly what the next phase of the run has to decide.

But I do not stop at that snapshot, because the whole point is to test whether interpolation is the end of learning. I plan to keep optimizing for orders of magnitude longer than the point where the training set is already fit, tracking training and validation accuracy on the same axis throughout. Two outcomes discriminate the competing readings: if delayed generalization is real, the held-out curve should stay flat for a long stretch after the training curve saturates and then begin rising later, eventually reaching the same near-perfect level as training even though the training loss itself is no longer moving; if the memorizing solution is actually the end state, the held-out curve should simply stay flat for as long as I keep training. The decision rule is the late shape of that curve — a rise with no matching change on the training side is delayed generalization after memorization, and a curve that never moves is not.

The loss curves should make the distinction sharper than accuracy alone, if the hypothesis is right. I expect training loss to fall early, and validation loss to first rise — a confidently wrong model on held-out entries is penalized harder than an uncertain one — before eventually descending again once generalization sets in. That predicted shape resembles double descent in loss, so I need a test that separates the two ideas rather than one that just notes the resemblance. The double-descent ancestors vary model capacity or effective capacity around interpolation thresholds, and even epoch-wise double descent is usually a peak in test error near the point of fitting. My setup instead fixes the model and task and varies nothing but optimization time, so the discriminating prediction is that held-out accuracy should be monotone rather than peaked, and any second descent in validation loss should land far after training loss has already gone flat — not clustered near the fitting boundary the way double descent's is. Seeing a peak-and-recover right at the fitting point instead would mean I have rediscovered double descent, not isolated a separate training-time transition.

I next need to know whether the delay is a curiosity of one split or a controllable dimension, and data fraction is the cleanest knob to sweep. Ordinary intuition says that reducing the number of revealed entries should hurt final generalization outright — that is the null hypothesis. The alternative worth testing is that the training fraction trades off against optimization time rather than against the ceiling itself: final validation accuracy staying close to its maximum across a range of fractions, while the number of steps needed to reach it grows sharply as the fraction shrinks, with the time to fit the training set staying comparatively flat or even shrinking over the same range. Sweeping the training fraction on a task such as S_5 product and tracking median time-to-threshold on the two curves separately is the discriminating test: a fraction that mainly changes the ceiling supports the ordinary intuition, a fraction that mainly changes the time-to-threshold under a matched step budget supports the alternative, and either way the effect should be sharpest near the smallest fraction that still generalizes at all within the budget.

If that pattern holds, it also tells me why the experiment has to be long. A short run would systematically mislabel the model as a memorizer, since the optimizer could spend a huge interval on a solution that fits training examples but does not yet implement the rule, with only the later held-out behavior able to reveal that a different internal solution has taken over. Training budget is therefore not a nuisance hyperparameter to minimize; it is part of the scientific object being measured.

I want internal checks that the task really behaves as an abstract-symbol problem, not something I only trust because a headline curve looks right. Modular arithmetic gives me one for free: for prime p, every nonzero residue mod p is a power of a primitive root, so multiplication mod p is isomorphic to addition mod p - 1 under a relabeling, and division matches subtraction under the same relabeling. With opaque symbols, the model should have no way to distinguish those structures except through the pattern of the operation table itself, so the prediction is that the data requirements for learning `x - y` and `x / y` should come out close to each other — if they diverge sharply, that would point to the setup exploiting some notation quirk rather than measuring symbol interactions. Two more predictions follow the same logic: symmetric operations should be easier to learn than related non-symmetric ones, since a transformer can cheaply ignore operand order when the rule doesn't depend on it, and I should expect at least some operations to look effectively random to the model within any reasonable step budget — if every rule turns out learnable, that would be a sign the task isn't hard enough to be a real test.

Now I ask what would push the optimizer from the memorizing solution toward the generalizing one, and the natural test is an intervention sweep on a task such as S_5 product: the same setup run with weight decay on and off, with decay pulling toward zero versus toward the saved initial weights, with and without minibatch or injected weight/update noise, and with the learning rate varied, all at matched step budgets. My leading hypothesis is that weight decay is the standout intervention, cutting the amount of training data needed to reach generalization within budget by more than any single alternative. Within that, I want to separate two priors that both predict decay should help, but for different reasons: "stay low-norm" (decay to zero) and "stay near where you started" (decay to initialization). If decay to the origin still comes out ahead of decay to initialization, that would rule out "stay near where you started" as the whole explanation and point to something about the trajectory's geometry, not just its distance from init, doing the work — and that head-to-head comparison, not either mode's score in isolation, is the decision rule for which explanation survives.

The same sharpness idea makes a second, independent prediction I can check. Prior work on noisy small-batch training suggests it biases optimization away from sharp minima and toward flatter regions, so if minibatch noise and injected Gaussian weight or update noise turn out to help generalization the same way weight decay is predicted to, that would be consistent with the memorizing table-lookup solution being sharp or high-norm and the rule-like solution being flatter or lower-norm, with long optimization under decay and noise gradually walking the model from one basin to the other. The check that actually discriminates this is a correlation, not just a direction: across S_5 runs stopped at a fixed time, compute a sharpness proxy — sensitivity of the loss to small parameter perturbations — alongside validation accuracy for each run, and look at the Spearman correlation between them. A negative, statistically significant correlation is the predicted signature, runs with flatter minima being the ones further along toward generalization. That would not be proof of the mechanism, but its absence would be a real problem for the sharpness story, which is what makes it a fair test rather than a foregone conclusion.

I also need to rule out the easy capacity story before trusting any of the above: maybe the model would generalize only because it lacks the capacity to memorize exceptions, in which case the mechanism claims above would be beside the point. The test is to corrupt a controlled number of training answers — replacing them with wrong-but-plausible values — and see whether the network still reaches full training accuracy on the corrupted set, fitting the false labels along with the true ones, and does so at roughly the same rate regardless of how many labels were corrupted. If it does, that would show the model has plenty of spare capacity to memorize both rule-consistent examples and explicit lies, ruling out incapacity as the reason generalization ever happens. I'd also want the corruption count to interact with generalization the way this story predicts: a small number of outliers should barely move whether a run generalizes at all, while a large number should shrink the range of training fractions that still do. Whatever the run shows, the case for "generalization is a separate solution optimization favors, not something forced by low capacity" stands or falls on training accuracy staying near total even with corrupted labels present.

Finally I want evidence that the held-out accuracy corresponds to a real learned structure rather than some brittle interpolation trick, and the output-layer embeddings are where I would look for it. My prediction for modular addition is that, if the model has actually recovered the group structure, the symbol embeddings should arrange into something recognizably circular, with adding a fixed residue tracing a consistent walk around that circle rather than landing in unrelated regions of embedding space; for S_5 the analogous prediction is that embeddings should cluster along cosets of a subgroup or its conjugates rather than sitting in generic high-dimensional positions. Structure that specific would be hard to explain as a high-dimensional accident and would instead read as a recognizable trace of the algebra the model is supposed to have found. If weight decay really pushes toward the flatter, rule-like solution the intervention sweep predicts, I would also expect embeddings from decayed runs to show this structure more cleanly than embeddings from undecayed runs — a second, independent place for that hypothesis to hold up or fail.

If the predictions above hold together, the payoff is more than "train longer." The experiment is designed as a microscope for a hidden transition: a network first finds a fast memorizing solution, then, under the right data fraction and optimizer pressures, moves toward a structured representation that implements the operation, with the held-out curve turning that internal transition into an observable event. By using abstract-symbol operation tables and training far past interpolation, the design lets memorization time and rule-discovery time be measured separately instead of collapsed into a single "did it fit" number — and the decision rule is the same one throughout: does the held-out curve move on its own, long after the training curve has stopped moving, or doesn't it.
