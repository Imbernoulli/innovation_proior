Let me first compress the problem to its minimal form. To prove that every ideal in a multivariate polynomial ring is finitely generated, I don't need to handle all variables at once; it suffices to show that R Noetherian implies R[x] Noetherian, and then I can add variables one at a time by repeating this. So the real question is: given an arbitrary ideal I in R[x], how do I extract finitely many generators from what looks like an infinite set of polynomials?

Looking directly at all the polynomials in I gives me no finiteness. The degrees can keep growing, and the generators could seemingly be infinite in number. But every nonzero polynomial has a leading term, and the coefficient of that leading term lives back in the base ring R. This is the key point: I don't know whether R[x] is Noetherian, but I do know that R is. So I collect all the leading coefficients of elements of I, obtaining an ideal L in R. Since R is Noetherian, L has finitely many generators, and these generators can be chosen to be the leading coefficients of some f_1,...,f_r in I.

Let d be the maximum degree among these f_i. If I face some h in I with degree at least d, its leading coefficient lies in L, so it can be written as a finite linear combination of the lc(f_i). Then I multiply each f_i by a suitable power of x so that their leading terms all align to degree deg h, multiply by the corresponding coefficients, and add them up. This new polynomial lies in I, and its leading term equals the leading term of h. After subtracting, the leading term cancels and the degree strictly drops.

This is the engine of the proof: high-degree elements are not generated directly, but are pushed down in degree through the finite control exercised by the leading coefficients. As long as the degree is still not less than d, the same elimination can be repeated. The degree, being a natural number, cannot decrease indefinitely, so it must eventually fall into the part with degree less than d.

What remains is the low-degree part. Polynomials of degree < d, viewed as an R-module, form nothing more than a finite free module R + Rx + ... + Rx^{d-1}. Because R is Noetherian, a finitely generated R-module is also Noetherian, so its submodule I cap R[x]_{<d} has finitely many generators. So the whole of I is generated by two kinds of finite data: one is the finitely many f_i controlling all the high-degree leading coefficients, the other is the finitely many module generators of the low-degree remainder.

If I switch to the language of Noetherian induction, the same contradiction becomes visible. Suppose there existed a non-finitely-generated ideal; then I could keep choosing, from among the elements not lying in the already-generated ideal, the one of smallest degree. Their leading coefficients generate an ascending chain in R; this chain must stabilize. Once it stabilizes, the newest leading coefficient is already generated by the earlier ones, so I can align degrees with the earlier polynomials and cancel the leading term, producing a counterexample of even lower degree, contradicting the choice of "minimal degree." This contradiction-based version exposes more directly the core trick of Hilbert's argument: if an infinite failure existed, it would manufacture a non-stabilizing ascending chain in the base ring; but the Noetherian property forbids such a chain.

So what the theorem actually proves is not "I can solve these equations," but "the algebraic consequences of all the equations can always be controlled by finitely many equations." This step shifts the center of gravity of algebra. Explicitly finding roots and eliminating variables are still important, but the foundational guarantee given by the Hilbert Basis Theorem is more abstract: in a polynomial ring, an ideal, as a structural object, is finitely controllable. As long as this finiteness holds in the base ring, it still holds after adjoining one variable; and it still holds after adjoining finitely many variables.

This is also its significance for algebraic geometry. An affine algebraic set can be defined by all the elements of some ideal, which looks like a zero set jointly defined by infinitely many equations. But if the polynomial ring is Noetherian, this ideal is finitely generated, so the same zero set is already determined by finitely many equations. Algebraic geometry can therefore replace an "infinite family of equations" with a "finitely generated ideal" and a "finitely presented coordinate ring." Hilbert's insight was to turn an infinite computational object into a finite structural object, and leading coefficients plus Noetherian induction are exactly the mechanism that accomplishes this transformation.
