# Hilbert Basis Theorem

**Theorem.** If `R` is a Noetherian commutative ring, then `R[x]` is also Noetherian. Hence for any finite number of variables, `R[x_1,...,x_n]` is also Noetherian. In particular, if `k` is a field, then every ideal of `k[x_1,...,x_n]` is finitely generated.

## Proof Sketch

Take any ideal `I subset R[x]`. Let `L` be the ideal of `R` generated by the leading coefficients of all nonzero polynomials in `I`. Since `R` is Noetherian, `L` has finitely many generators; choose `f_1,...,f_r in I` such that `lc(f_1),...,lc(f_r)` generate `L`. Let

```text
d = max_i deg(f_i).
```

Now consider the low-degree part

```text
M = I cap {polynomials of degree < d}.
```

Polynomials of degree `< d` form the finitely generated `R`-module `R + Rx + ... + Rx^{d-1}`, and its submodule `M` is also finitely generated. Choose `g_1,...,g_s` generating `M`.

Now we show that `I` is generated by the finite set

```text
f_1,...,f_r, g_1,...,g_s
```

Take any `h in I`. If `deg h < d`, then `h in M`, already generated by the `g_j`. If `deg h >= d`, since `lc(h) in L`, it can be written as

```text
lc(h) = sum_i a_i lc(f_i).
```

Multiply each `f_i` by `x^{deg h - deg f_i}` weighted by `a_i` to obtain a polynomial belonging to `(f_1,...,f_r)` whose leading term equals the leading term of `h`. Subtracting gives `h' in I` with `deg h' < deg h`. Inducting on the degree eventually pushes `h` down into the low-degree part, so `h` belongs to `(f_1,...,f_r,g_1,...,g_s)`. Hence `I` is finitely generated, and `R[x]` is Noetherian.

The multivariate result follows from repeatedly applying the one-step theorem.

## Core Insight

The distinctive insight of the Hilbert Basis Theorem is not to give a root-finding algorithm, but to split an infinite-generation problem into a finite-control problem:

- The high-degree terms are controlled by the leading-coefficient ideal in `R`.
- The low-degree terms are controlled inside a finitely generated `R`-module.
- The degree-lowering process is guaranteed to terminate by the well-ordering of natural-number degree, or equivalently by the minimal-counterexample method of Noetherian induction.

In the language of minimal counterexamples: if `I` could not be finitely generated, one would keep choosing, among elements not lying in the already-generated ideal, the one of minimal degree. Their leading coefficients generate an ascending chain in `R`; the Noetherian property forces this chain to stabilize. Once it stabilizes, the leading term of the new polynomial can be canceled using the earlier polynomials, producing a counterexample of even lower degree — a contradiction.

## Why It Changed Algebra

Before this theorem, polynomial algebra was easily understood as a technique for "solving equations": write down equations, eliminate variables, find common zeros. Hilbert's proof shows that the more fundamental issue is finiteness: even if a family of equations is infinite, the ideal it generates may still be controlled by finitely many elements; even without solving explicitly, one can prove that the structure cannot spiral out of control.

This shifted algebra from the explicit, equation-by-equation handling of individual equations toward ideals, modules, quotient rings, chain conditions, and finite presentation. That adjoining one variable still preserves finite generation means that finitely-generated algebras and affine algebraic geometry have a reliable finite description: infinitely many algebraic consequences can be encoded by a finitely generated ideal, and an affine algebraic set can likewise be defined by finitely many equations.
